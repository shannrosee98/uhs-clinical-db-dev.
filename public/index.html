Pain

STUDY
PAIN

Pain reference

Score it, manage it, and know the doses — three tabs covering the whole picture.

0No pain
1–3Mild
4–6Moderate
7–9Severe
10Worst pain imaginable

Ask

“On a scale of 0 to 10, where 0 is no pain and 10 is the worst pain you can imagine, what is your pain right now?”

“Where is the pain?” “When did it start?” “What does it feel like?” “Does it move anywhere?” “What makes it better or worse?”

Reassess

Record the score before treatment and again afterwards. A useful RP note is: pain 8/10 → treatment → pain 4/10, together with the patient's clinical observations and response.

Important: pain severity alone does not determine which medication is safe. NICE recommends regular pain assessment and appropriate analgesia, with treatment selected according to the injury and patient factors.

Start with the cause, not just the number

A 9/10 headache, 9/10 abdominal pain, 9/10 chest pain and 9/10 fracture pain are not automatically treated identically. Assess ABCDE and look for the underlying cause before reaching for the ladder below — the score guides intensity, not the diagnosis.

Non-pharmacological options first (or alongside)

Splinting/immobilising an injury, positioning the patient comfortably, reassurance and calm communication, applying cold packs where appropriate, and simply reducing unnecessary movement all reduce pain — and cost nothing, carry no side effects, and work while you're preparing medication.

ADULT ACUTE PAIN • RP REFERENCE

What analgesia fits the pain score?

Use the score as a guide only. The injury, observations, age, weight, allergies, pregnancy, renal/hepatic function, bleeding risk and existing medicines can change the choice.

1–3

Mild

First-line

Paracetamol 500 mg–1 g PO per dose, at least 4 hours apart.

Maximum usual adult dose: 4 g in 24 hours. Consider a lower maximum in people under 50 kg or with relevant risk factors.

Ibuprofen 200–400 mg PO can be considered when an NSAID is appropriate; common OTC maximum 1.2 g/day.

4–6

Moderate

Step up if required

Paracetamol 500 mg–1 g PO remains a base analgesic where appropriate.

For appropriate acute injuries/fractures, NICE recommends paracetamol + codeine for moderate pain. Co-codamol contains 500 mg paracetamol + 8, 15 or 30 mg codeine per tablet; adults can take 1–2 tablets up to 4 times/day, with 4–6 hours between doses and a maximum of 8 tablets/day.

Important: count the paracetamol in co-codamol toward the daily paracetamol maximum.

7–9

Severe

Stronger analgesia / escalation

For major trauma, NICE recommends IV morphine as first-line, adjusted to effect by an appropriately trained clinician.

A historical NICE acute-trauma evidence dosing approach describes an initial up to 5 mg IV, then 1–5 mg IV increments at 5-minute intervals, titrated to effect. This is not a universal fixed dose and requires appropriate monitoring and clinical authority.

For suspected long-bone fracture, NICE recommends IV paracetamol supplemented with IV morphine titrated to effect for severe pain.

10/10

Extreme / uncontrolled

Emergency assessment

Do not simply keep increasing tablets because the score is 10. Find and treat the cause, repeat ABCDE/observations and escalate.

For major trauma, use the severe-pain pathway with appropriately titrated IV opioid analgesia and monitoring. Consider specialist/anaesthetic support where pain remains uncontrolled.

Reassess pain, respiratory rate, SpO₂, consciousness and BP after opioid treatment.

Reassess, every time

Record the score before treatment and again afterwards — pain 8/10 → treatment → pain 4/10 is a genuinely useful RP note. If a treatment isn't working, escalate rather than repeating the same thing.

Escalate when

Pain remains severe/uncontrolled despite appropriate treatment, there are signs of a serious underlying cause, or the patient needs analgesia beyond what's within your own scope — hand over to a senior clinician rather than pushing further alone.

Head & Face

  • Inspect scalp, skull, face and eyes for injury, swelling, bleeding or deformity.
  • Check pupils and neurological status as clinically appropriate.
  • Assess mouth, teeth, tongue and facial symmetry.
  • Look for signs of fluid or blood from the ears/nose when relevant.

Neck

  • Inspect for wounds, swelling, bruising and penetrating injury.
  • Assess tracheal position and neck tenderness where appropriate.
  • Reassess cervical-spine concerns in trauma patients and maintain precautions where indicated.

Chest

  • Inspect chest movement, wounds, bruising and deformity.
  • Palpate for tenderness, instability and crepitus where appropriate.
  • Auscultate breath sounds when indicated and compare sides.
  • Reassess oxygenation and respiratory effort.

Abdomen & Pelvis

  • Inspect for distension, bruising, wounds and visible bleeding.
  • Assess abdominal tenderness systematically and note guarding/rigidity where relevant.
  • Assess the pelvis cautiously in trauma and avoid repeated unnecessary manipulation.
  • Look for evidence of external bleeding or other injuries.

Limbs

  • Inspect each limb for deformity, swelling, wounds, burns and bleeding.
  • Assess distal circulation, sensation and movement.
  • Compare sides and document any neurovascular deficit.
  • Check for injuries that may be hidden by clothing/equipment.

Back & Spine

  • Inspect the back when clinically necessary, using an appropriate team-assisted log roll.
  • Look for wounds, bruising, deformity and pressure-related injury.
  • Assess spinal tenderness and neurological symptoms where appropriate.
  • Minimise movement when spinal injury remains a concern.

Emergency presentations and response reference

STUDY
HIGH PRIORITY

Emergency quick reference

Cardiac arrest

Check for no more than 10 seconds — unresponsive and not breathing normally means starting CPR immediately. Compress at 100–120/min, 5–6cm depth, minimal interruptions. Attach the AED/defibrillator as soon as available and follow its prompts. See the Cardiac page for the full arrest algorithm, drugs and RP library.

Severe bleeding

Apply firm direct pressure with a dressing first. If a limb bleed continues, apply a tourniquet high and tight and note the application time. Consider haemostatic packing for junctional or non-limb wounds. Reassess for shock — pulse, skin colour, capillary refill, BP — and escalate rapidly.

Stroke

Use FAST — Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time symptoms started — to screen quickly. Check capillary glucose to rule out hypoglycaemia as a mimic. Establish the exact time last seen well, since it determines thrombolysis/thrombectomy eligibility, and pre-alert the stroke unit.

Anaphylaxis

Look for sudden airway swelling, stridor, wheeze or persistent hypotension alongside a likely trigger. Give IM adrenaline into the anterolateral thigh without delay. Lie the patient flat with legs raised (or sitting if breathless), and repeat adrenaline after 5 minutes if there's no improvement.

Seizure

Note the exact start time, move hazards away and cushion the head rather than restraining the patient. Most seizures self-terminate within 2–3 minutes. A seizure lasting beyond 5 minutes, or repeating without recovery in between, is status epilepticus and needs urgent escalation for benzodiazepine treatment.

Chest pain

Take a SOCRATES-style pain history, obtain a 12-lead ECG within 10 minutes where possible, and give oxygen only if hypoxic. See the Cardiac page for the full ACS assessment pathway, RP library and medication doses.

Sepsis

Suspect sepsis where there's a likely infection source plus a red flag — new confusion, systolic BP ≤90, HR >130, RR ≥25, non-blanching rash, or no urine output in 18 hours. Start the "Sepsis Six" within the hour where within scope: oxygen, cultures, IV antibiotics, IV fluids, lactate and urine output monitoring.

Head Injury

Repeat GCS matters more than a single reading — watch the trend, and keep spinal precautions in mind given the mechanism.

Recognise

Any loss of consciousness, amnesia, vomiting, seizure activity, severe headache, GCS below 15, or a focal neurological deficit.

Signs of skull fracture

Battle's sign (bruising behind the ear), "panda eyes" (bruising around the eyes), CSF leak from the nose or ear, or haemotympanum.

Urgent CT criteria

GCS below 13 initially, GCS below 15 at 2 hours, suspected open/depressed skull fracture, signs of a basal skull fracture, a post-traumatic seizure, focal neurological deficit, more than one episode of vomiting, or being on anticoagulants with any head injury.

🫀

Chest Injury

An open chest wound and a flail segment both need specific handling — don't just treat every chest injury the same way.

Recognise

Pain worse on breathing, bruising or wounds to the chest wall, paradoxical (flail) chest wall movement, reduced or absent breath sounds on one side, tracheal deviation, or surgical emphysema (crackling under the skin).

Key patterns

Flail chest, simple or tension pneumothorax, haemothorax, open (sucking) chest wound, pulmonary contusion, and rib fractures.

Open chest wound

Cover with a dressing sealed on three sides, allowing air to escape but not re-enter, and watch closely for an evolving tension pneumothorax.

🩻

Abdominal & Pelvic Trauma

A normal-looking abdomen early on doesn't rule out serious internal bleeding — reassess repeatedly.

Recognise

Abdominal tenderness, guarding or distension, bruising (including a seatbelt mark), pelvic pain or instability, blood at the urethral meatus, or a leg length discrepancy.

Pelvic fracture

Handle gently — avoid repeatedly "springing" or manipulating the pelvis, which can dislodge a forming clot and worsen bleeding. Apply a pelvic binder at the level of the greater trochanters if indicated.

Solid organ injury

Spleen or liver injury can cause substantial concealed blood loss while the abdomen still looks relatively normal early on.

🦴

Spinal Injury

Once spinal injury is suspected, minimise movement and keep the spine aligned until it's safe to clear.

Consider the mechanism

Falls from height, diving injuries, RTCs, direct blows to the spine, or any significant axial-loading mechanism.

Assess for

Spinal tenderness, deformity or a step on gentle palpation, and any neurological symptoms — weakness, numbness, tingling, or loss of bladder/bowel control.

Log roll

Used to examine the back and move the patient onto a board/scoop while keeping the spine aligned — needs a coordinated team, with one person controlling the head and neck.

🦾

Joint Dislocations

Often obvious on inspection, but the real priority is checking what's happening below the joint — never skip the neurovascular check.

Shoulder

The most commonly dislocated joint, usually anteriorly, from a fall onto an outstretched arm or a direct blow. The arm is typically held slightly away from the body and resists movement — check the "regimental badge" area on the outer shoulder for axillary nerve sensation.

Finger

Usually at an interphalangeal joint from a direct blow or hyperextension — a "jammed finger." Deformity is often obvious; check circulation and sensation before and after any reduction attempt.

Elbow

Usually posterior, from a fall onto an outstretched hand. Document the radial pulse and hand sensation before and after, given the risk to the brachial artery and nerves running close by.

Hip

Most commonly posterior — classically a "dashboard injury" in a road traffic collision. The leg is typically shortened, flexed and internally rotated. Time-critical, given the risk to the blood supply of the femoral head.

Patella (kneecap)

Usually dislocates laterally, often during a sporting twisting injury, with the kneecap visibly displaced to the outer side of the knee.

Ankle

Often accompanies a fracture (fracture-dislocation). Look for gross deformity and check circulation urgently, since the skin can be at risk from pressure over a displaced bone.

Jaw (TMJ)

Can follow a wide yawn, a direct blow, or dental treatment. The patient is often unable to close their mouth and may have difficulty speaking.

General principles

Dislocations are often obvious on inspection, but always check and document distal circulation, sensation and movement before and after any reduction attempt. Reduction should only be attempted by someone trained and appropriately supervised, within their scope — support and splint rather than attempt reduction otherwise.

FRACTURE GUIDE

Fractures & broken bones

A complete visual study reference — patterns, recognition, assessment, management, red flags and healing — plus a structured checklist and /me + F8 RP library.

Transverse

The fracture line runs approximately straight across the bone. Usually caused by a direct blow or a force applied at a right angle to the bone.

Linear

A crack running parallel to the bone's long axis rather than across it. Often from a direct impact, and can be easy to miss on a quick look.

Oblique (non-displaced)

The fracture line crosses the bone at an angle, with fragments remaining in reasonable alignment.

Oblique (displaced)

The same angled break pattern, but the fragments have shifted out of alignment — often visibly deformed, and usually needing reduction.

Spiral

A twisting force creates a fracture line that winds around the bone. Common in sporting injuries, and a pattern worth being alert to when the mechanism described doesn't fit, particularly in children.

Greenstick

An incomplete fracture in which one side bends while the other side breaks. Seen almost exclusively in children, whose bones are more flexible than an adult's.

Comminuted

The bone is divided into three or more major fragments, usually from high-energy trauma such as a fall from height or a road traffic collision.

Segmental

Two separate fracture lines create an isolated segment of bone trapped between them.

Buckle / torus

The bone cortex buckles or compresses without a complete break. Commonly seen in children.

Impacted

One fracture fragment is driven into another, often compacting and shortening the bone slightly.

Compression

The bone is crushed or compressed, often producing a shortened or collapsed appearance — typically seen in the spine.

Avulsion

A small piece of bone is pulled away by a tendon or ligament, usually at the point where it attaches.

Displacement & alignment

A non-displaced fracture remains substantially aligned, whereas a displaced fracture has fragments that have moved. Clinicians describe this using translation, angulation, shortening and rotation — alignment matters because it affects stability, healing, and nerve or vessel function.

Open vs closed fractures

A closed fracture doesn't communicate with the outside through a wound. An open fracture has a wound communicating with the fracture site — bone may or may not be visible — and is urgent because of contamination and infection risk. Never push exposed bone back in or attempt to straighten the limb; control bleeding without pressing on exposed bone, cover the wound, and keep the limb as still as possible.

Recognising a suspected fracture

Pain (especially with movement/loading), swelling or bruising, deformity or an abnormal angle, reduced or abnormal movement, loss of normal function, shortening or rotation, an associated wound or bleeding, numbness or tingling, and abnormal colour or temperature beyond the injury. The appearance of an injury can't reliably confirm or exclude a fracture — a person may still be able to move an injured limb despite one. Don't deliberately test for crepitus or instability.

Neurovascular assessment — P-C-C-M-S

Pulse — assess distal pulse, compare with the unaffected side. Colour — check for pallor, blue/grey colour or other changes. Capillary refill — assess distal perfusion where appropriate. Movement — gentle movement only when safe, never force a painful/deformed injury. Sensation — check for numbness, tingling or altered sensation distal to the injury. Record findings before and after immobilisation, and report any deterioration promptly.

Immobilisation principles

Support the injured limb rather than repeatedly manipulating it. Support the joints above and below the suspected fracture where practical, and use padding to protect pressure points. Don't force a deformed limb straight or push exposed bone beneath the skin. Remove rings, watches and other constrictive items early, since swelling can increase. Recheck circulation and sensation after applying support or a splint.

Diagnosis & X-ray terminology

Diagnosis combines history, mechanism, examination and imaging when indicated. AP (anteroposterior) — the X-ray beam passes front to back. Lateral — a side projection. Oblique — an angled projection used when extra detail is needed. CT/MRI may be used for selected injuries.

Casting

A cast is only appropriate for limb fractures that are stable and non-displaced (or reduced first) — not for ribs, spine, skull or most shoulder injuries. Apply padding before the casting material, hold the joint in the correct functional position while it sets, and never wrap it too tight. Check it fits two fingers comfortably at the top, elevate the limb afterwards, and give clear cast-care advice (keep dry, don't insert objects, seek help urgently for worsening pain, numbness or colour change).

Acute compartment syndrome: a time-critical condition where pressure rises inside a closed muscle compartment and threatens blood flow and tissue viability. Watch for rapidly increasing or severe pain — particularly pain disproportionate to the apparent injury — pain with passive stretch, tense/markedly swollen tissue, numbness or pins and needles, and weakness. Don't wait for a late absent pulse before taking action — suspected compartment syndrome needs urgent emergency assessment.

When to call 999

Open fracture or visible bone, severe/uncontrolled bleeding, major deformity or suspected severe displacement, absent or markedly reduced distal circulation, new significant numbness/weakness, signs of shock, suspected spinal/pelvic/major long-bone injury, severe head injury or altered consciousness, breathing difficulty, or possible compartment syndrome.

Complications

Compartment syndrome, nerve injury, vascular injury, infection (especially open fractures), fat embolism syndrome (rare, associated with major long-bone trauma), delayed union/non-union, malunion, stiffness/reduced function, and post-traumatic arthritis in joint-surface injuries.

Clavicle

Often follows a fall or impact to the shoulder. Pain, swelling and deformity may occur.

Humerus

Upper-arm fractures can affect nearby nerves and vessels, so distal neurovascular checks are important.

Wrist / distal radius

Often occurs after a fall onto an outstretched hand; swelling and deformity may be present.

Hip / femoral neck

Particularly important in older adults after low-energy falls — inability to bear weight and a shortened/externally-rotated leg can occur.

A major long-bone injury that can be associated with substantial blood loss.

Ankle

Bone healing

Inflammatory: a haematoma forms, inflammatory signalling begins. Repair/soft callus: fibrous and cartilaginous tissue bridges the fracture. Hard callus/consolidation: new bone progressively strengthens the site. Remodelling: bone is reshaped over time. Healing time varies with the bone, pattern, blood supply, age, smoking, nutrition and treatment.

Airway Management

Work up the airway ladder — always use the simplest device that does the job, escalating only when it isn't enough.

The airway ladder

Positioning & basic manoeuvres → basic adjuncts (OPA/NPA) → supraglottic device (i-gel/LMA) → definitive airway (ETT) → surgical airway. Move up only as far as needed, and always be ready to step back down if a simpler option becomes adequate again.

Basic manoeuvres

Head-tilt, chin-lift — first-line for a medical patient with no trauma concern. Jaw thrust — used instead where cervical spine injury is suspected, since it avoids neck extension; push the angles of the jaw forward with both hands while a colleague stabilises the neck.

Oropharyngeal airway (OPA / Guedel)

Holds the tongue off the posterior pharyngeal wall. Only tolerated by a deeply unconscious patient with no gag reflex — if they gag, it's the wrong choice.

  • Sizing: measure from the corner of the mouth to the angle of the jaw (or the earlobe)
  • Size 000–00: neonate
  • Size 0–1: infant/small child
  • Size 2: child / small adult
  • Size 3: average adult female
  • Size 4: average adult male
  • Size 5: large adult
  • Insertion (adult): concave-up, rotate 180° as it passes the soft palate
  • Insertion (child): right-way-up under direct vision with a tongue depressor — don't rotate, the smaller mouth risks soft-tissue injury

Nasopharyngeal airway (NPA)

Better tolerated than an OPA in a patient with some gag reflex remaining.

  • Sizing: diameter roughly matches the patient's little finger/nostril
  • ~6mm: smaller adult
  • ~7mm: average adult
  • ~8mm: larger adult
  • Length: nostril to the tragus of the ear, plus a safety pin/flange so it can't disappear into the nose
  • Avoid with a suspected base-of-skull fracture or significant mid-face trauma

Supraglottic airway (i-gel / LMA)

Sits above the vocal cords — a better seal than basic adjuncts, without needing laryngoscopy. A step up from basic airways, a step below full intubation.

  • Sizing is by patient weight:
  • Size 1: <5kg (neonate)
  • Size 1.5: 5–12kg (infant)
  • Size 2: 10–25kg (small child)
  • Size 2.5: 25–35kg (large child)
  • Size 3: 30–60kg (small adult)
  • Size 4: 50–90kg (medium/large adult)
  • Size 5: >90kg (large adult)
  • Insertion: lubricate, slight neck extension (unless trauma), slide along the hard palate in one smooth movement until resistance is felt

Endotracheal tube (ETT)

The definitive airway — protects against aspiration and allows full ventilatory control.

  • Adult sizing (internal diameter, cuffed): 7.0–7.5mm average female, 7.5–8.5mm average male
  • Paediatric sizing (uncuffed): (age in years ÷ 4) + 4 — for a cuffed tube, subtract roughly 0.5
  • Depth at the lips (oral): approximately 3 × the internal diameter (e.g. a 7.5mm tube sits around 22–23cm) — always confirm with capnography, and a chest X-ray in hospital
  • Cuff pressure: keep within roughly 20–30 cmH₂O to avoid tracheal mucosal damage

Surgical airway (cricothyroidotomy)

Last resort for a "can't intubate, can't oxygenate" emergency, when no other airway can be secured.

  • Landmark: the cricothyroid membrane, between the thyroid and cricoid cartilage
  • Typical adult tube size: approximately 6mm internal diameter for a surgical technique
  • Rare, high-stakes, and requires specific training — not a routine step on the ladder

Cardiac assessment & care

A structured UK ambulance-style approach to suspected cardiac chest pain and cardiac arrest, for RP realism. This is not real clinical guidance — follow your actual training and your service's own protocols.

❤

Suspected cardiac chest pain / ACS

SOCRATES-based chest pain history, early 12-lead ECG, oxygen only if hypoxic, aspirin where indicated, and early escalation of red flags.

Recognise ACS

Central/retrosternal chest pain or discomfort, often heavy, tight or crushing, which may radiate to the arm, jaw, neck or back. May come with sweating, nausea, breathlessness or a sense of impending doom. Older patients, women and people with diabetes can present atypically with little or no pain.

Oxygen & monitoring

Current UK guidance does not support routine high-flow oxygen in a normoxic patient — only give supplemental oxygen if SpO₂ is below the target range (commonly under 94%, lower in known COPD). Attach continuous cardiac monitoring and repeat observations regularly.

12-lead ECG

Obtain and review a 12-lead ECG as early as possible, ideally within 10 minutes of first contact. Look for ST-segment elevation or depression, T-wave inversion and any new left bundle branch block. Compare against a previous ECG where one is available.

Escalate early

Ongoing pain despite treatment, a dynamic or abnormal ECG, haemodynamic instability or a new arrhythmia are red flags. Escalate to a senior clinician/cardiology promptly and pre-alert the receiving unit.

ECG territories

Inferior changes (II, III, aVF) suggest the right coronary artery; anterior changes (V1–V4) suggest the LAD; lateral changes (I, aVL, V5–V6) suggest the circumflex. Reciprocal ST depression in the opposite leads supports a genuine STEMI over a mimic.

Risk stratification

Tools like the HEART score (ED chest pain risk) and GRACE score (in-hospital/6-month risk in confirmed ACS) exist locally to help guide admission, early discharge or invasive management decisions alongside clinical judgement.

🚑

Cardiac arrest & resuscitation

Early recognition, high-quality CPR, early defibrillation and treating reversible causes give the patient the best chance of a good outcome.

Recognise arrest

Unresponsive and not breathing normally, or only making agonal/gasping breaths. Check for no more than 10 seconds — don't delay starting compressions to keep re-checking.

Compressions

Centre of the chest, rate 100–120 per minute, depth 5–6cm, allowing full chest recoil between compressions. Minimise interruptions and swap the compressor role roughly every 2 minutes to maintain quality.

Defibrillation

Attach the AED/defibrillator as soon as it's available and follow its prompts. Stand clear during rhythm analysis and shock delivery, then resume compressions immediately afterwards.

Reversible causes

Consider the 4 Hs — Hypoxia, Hypovolaemia, Hypo/hyperkalaemia & metabolic causes, Hypothermia — and the 4 Ts — Thrombosis, Tamponade, Toxins, Tension pneumothorax.

Post-ROSC care

Target SpO₂ 94–98% (avoid hyperoxia) and normal CO₂ levels. Consider temperature management per local protocol, obtain a 12-lead ECG, and arrange urgent cardiology referral if a STEMI cause is likely.

Team dynamics

A clear team leader, closed-loop communication, and rotating compressors every 2 minutes keep quality high. Rhythm and pulse checks happen at each 2-minute cycle, in the pause immediately before or after a shock.

Stroke types and recognition

Ischaemic stroke

Brain injury caused by interruption of cerebral blood flow, commonly from thrombosis or embolism. Consider large-vessel occlusion (LVO) when there are severe focal deficits and follow the configured specialist pathway.

  • Face/arm/leg weakness or numbness
  • Speech or language disturbance
  • Visual loss or field symptoms
  • Neglect, gaze deviation or ataxia
  • Reduced consciousness in severe cases

Intracerebral haemorrhage

Bleeding within brain tissue. Sudden neurological deficit may occur with severe headache, vomiting, reduced consciousness, seizure or marked hypertension. Treat deterioration as an emergency and escalate.

Subarachnoid haemorrhage

Bleeding into the subarachnoid space. A thunderclap or sudden severe headache, collapse, vomiting, meningism, seizure or reduced consciousness are important red flags.

TIA

Transient neurological symptoms can resolve, but this does not make the event benign. NICE recommends immediate specialist assessment for suspected TIA and advises against using ABCD2 to determine urgency.

Stroke mimics

Consider hypoglycaemia, seizure/post-ictal weakness, migraine, Bell's palsy, intoxication, sepsis, syncope and functional neurological symptoms. A mimic should be investigated rather than assumed.

Red flags

Rapid neurological deterioration, falling GCS, severe headache at onset, seizure, anticoagulant use, vomiting, new pupillary abnormality, airway compromise or other critical ABCDE abnormality.

CT / CTA

NICE recommends immediate non-enhanced CT in specified higher-risk situations including potential thrombolysis/thrombectomy, anticoagulant use, bleeding tendency, depressed consciousness, progressive/fluctuating symptoms, papilloedema/neck stiffness/fever or severe headache at onset. Acute stroke patients should have swallowing screened by an appropriately trained healthcare professional before oral food, fluid or medication is given.

Stroke-like weakness with low glucose. Branch on treatment response and reassessment rather than assuming stroke.

07 — Post-seizure weakness

Transient focal weakness after a witnessed seizure. Branch on recovery, glucose and ongoing neurological deficit.

08 — Wake-up stroke

Symptoms found on waking. Record last-known-well before sleep separately from discovery time.

09 — Deteriorating stroke

Falling GCS and worsening airway/breathing. Prioritise ABCDE, escalation and reassessment.

Stroke Roleplay Addendum: Real Doses, Observations and Playable Pathways

> Roleplay disclaimer: The doses below are real adult clinical doses for reference, not instructions for real-world administration. In your FiveM server, they should only be used by the ranks authorised in your SOP. Real treatment depends on weight, renal function, timing, contraindications, monitoring and local policy.

NICE recommends urgent stroke assessment, glucose testing, CT/CTA, specialist stroke care and specialist-only thrombolysis. [1] &#x20;

1. Universal stroke observations

Every suspected stroke patient should have:

| Observation | Why it matters | | ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | | GCS/AVPU | Detects deterioration and airway risk | | Pupils | May suggest raised intracranial pressure or herniation | | Blood pressure | Important for thrombolysis and haemorrhage | | Heart rate/rhythm | Atrial fibrillation may indicate an embolic stroke | | Respiratory rate | Detects aspiration, fatigue or raised intracranial pressure | | SpO₂ | Determines whether oxygen is required | | Temperature | Fever worsens neurological injury | | Capillary blood glucose | Hypoglycaemia is a major stroke mimic | | FAST/NIHSS | Measures neurological deficit | | Limb strength | Detects progression or improvement | | Speech/language | Detects aphasia or dysarthria | | Facial symmetry | Detects cranial-nerve involvement | | Swallowing | Determines aspiration risk | | Headache/vomiting | More concerning for haemorrhage or raised intracranial pressure | | Seizure activity | May be a presentation or complication | | Anticoagulant history | Changes imaging, thrombolysis and reversal decisions |

NICE recommends oxygen only where oxygen saturation is low, and recommends swallowing assessment before oral food, fluids or medication. [1] &#x20;

2. How observations differ by stroke type

2.1 Ischaemic stroke

Typical observations

  • Sudden unilateral facial droop
  • Arm or leg weakness
  • Aphasia or dysarthria
  • Visual field defect
  • Neglect
  • Gaze deviation
  • Ataxia
  • Atrial fibrillation
  • Consciousness often preserved initially
  • Blood pressure often elevated
  • Glucose usually normal unless diabetes or a mimic is involved

Roleplay example

> BP 182/100, HR 112 irregular, GCS 14, glucose 6.4 mmol/L, right facial droop, left arm power 2/5, expressive aphasia, left visual neglect, NIHSS 15.

Observe for deterioration

  • New headache
  • New vomiting
  • Reduced GCS
  • New pupil asymmetry
  • Worsening weakness
  • Seizure
  • New hypertension or bradycardia

These may indicate haemorrhagic transformation, cerebral oedema or another complication.

2.2 Large-vessel occlusion

This is a severe ischaemic stroke involving a major cerebral artery.

Typical observations

  • Dense hemiplegia
  • Gaze deviation
  • Severe aphasia
  • Neglect
  • Visual field loss
  • NIHSS often above 6
  • Rapidly worsening symptoms
  • Reduced consciousness in severe cases

Roleplay example

> Patient has sudden right-sided paralysis, left gaze deviation, global aphasia, NIHSS 22 and GCS 12. CTA later shows proximal MCA occlusion.

Roleplay pathway

  • Pre-alert stroke centre
  • CT brain
  • CTA
  • CT perfusion if late presentation
  • Stroke consultant
  • Thrombectomy-centre referral
  • Thrombolysis if eligible and not contraindicated
  • Direct transfer to angiography or thrombectomy centre

NICE supports thrombectomy as soon as possible and, in selected patients, up to 24 hours after last-known-well when imaging demonstrates salvageable brain tissue. [1] &#x20;

2.3 Intracerebral haemorrhage

Typical observations

  • Severe headache
  • Vomiting
  • Rapidly worsening weakness
  • Reduced GCS
  • Seizure
  • Very high blood pressure
  • Unequal pupils
  • Abnormal breathing
  • Neck stiffness may occur
  • Cushing-type pattern in severe raised intracranial pressure:
    • Increasing blood pressure
    • Bradycardia
    • Irregular respiration

Roleplay example

> BP 226/118, HR 54, GCS 10 falling to 8, unequal pupils, vomiting, right-sided weakness and severe headache.

Roleplay pathway

  • ABCDE
  • Airway preparation
  • Urgent CT brain
  • No aspirin
  • No thrombolysis
  • Continuous BP monitoring
  • Anticoagulant reversal if indicated
  • Neurosurgical referral
  • ICU or hyperacute stroke-unit admission
  • Repeat CT if neurological deterioration occurs

NICE recommends considering rapid BP reduction in suitable intracerebral haemorrhage patients, aiming for approximately 140 mmHg systolic while avoiding an excessive fall. [1] &#x20;

2.4 Subarachnoid haemorrhage

Typical observations

  • Sudden thunderclap headache
  • Patient may describe it as “the worst headache of my life”
  • Vomiting
  • Photophobia
  • Neck stiffness
  • Collapse
  • Seizure
  • Reduced consciousness
  • Possible focal weakness
  • Possible pupil abnormality

Roleplay example

> Patient suddenly collapses with severe headache, vomiting, neck stiffness, BP 210/108 and GCS 13. No obvious facial droop, but they are confused and photophobic.

Roleplay pathway

  • ABCDE
  • Treat airway compromise
  • Urgent CT brain
  • CTA for aneurysm assessment
  • No aspirin or anticoagulant
  • Analgesia and antiemetic
  • Neurosurgical/neurovascular referral
  • ICU admission
  • Monitor for rebleeding, hydrocephalus and vasospasm

2.5 TIA

Typical observations

At assessment, the patient may appear almost normal.

  • FAST may be negative
  • GCS usually 15
  • No persistent weakness
  • No ongoing aphasia
  • Symptoms have resolved
  • Glucose may be normal
  • BP may remain elevated
  • There may be a history of recurrent transient episodes

Important documentation

  • Original symptoms
  • Exact onset
  • Duration
  • Exact resolution time
  • Witness account
  • Anticoagulants and antiplatelets
  • Previous TIA or stroke

Roleplay pathway

  • Immediate neurological assessment
  • Glucose and observations
  • Aspirin 300 mg if appropriate and not contraindicated
  • Urgent TIA/stroke specialist referral
  • Carotid imaging
  • MRI if indicated
  • No automatic discharge without specialist review

NICE recommends aspirin 300 mg immediately for suspected TIA unless contraindicated and specialist assessment within 24 hours. [1] &#x20;

2.6 Posterior circulation stroke

Typical observations

  • Vertigo
  • Vomiting
  • Diplopia
  • Dysarthria
  • Ataxia
  • Limb incoordination
  • Bilateral weakness
  • Abnormal eye movements
  • Reduced consciousness
  • Difficulty swallowing
  • Breathing compromise in severe brainstem stroke

FAST may be negative.

Roleplay example

> Patient has sudden vertigo, vomiting, double vision, inability to walk and slurred speech. Face and arms appear symmetrical. Glucose is normal.

Roleplay pathway

  • Treat as possible stroke despite negative FAST
  • Urgent CT and CTA
  • Consider MRI if CT is unrevealing
  • Stroke consultant review
  • Consider posterior-circulation thrombectomy if a basilar or vertebral occlusion is found

2.7 Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis

Typical observations

  • Headache
  • Seizure
  • Papilloedema
  • Visual disturbance
  • Focal deficit
  • Confusion
  • Raised intracranial pressure
  • May occur in pregnancy, postpartum state, infection or thrombophilia

Roleplay pathway

  • CT brain
  • CT venogram or MR venogram
  • Neurology/stroke consultant
  • Anticoagulation may be used under specialist direction, including when secondary haemorrhage is present

NICE recommends full-dose anticoagulation for cerebral venous sinus thrombosis unless contraindicated. [1] &#x20;

3. Real medication doses for your server formulary

3.1 Oxygen

Oxygen is not prescribed by a fixed “dose” in the same way as a tablet. It is titrated to oxygen saturation and clinical condition.

Suggested roleplay settings:

| Situation | Delivery | | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | | Mild hypoxia | Nasal cannula 2–4 L/min | | Moderate hypoxia | Simple face mask 5–10 L/min | | Severe hypoxia or airway emergency | Non-rebreather mask 10–15 L/min | | Ventilated patient | Bag-valve-mask with high-flow oxygen |

Give oxygen for hypoxia, not routinely to a patient with normal saturation. [1] &#x20;

/me opens the oxygen cylinder and checks the pressure gauge
/me connects the oxygen tubing to a non-rebreather mask
/me sets the oxygen flow to 15 litres per minute
/me applies the mask and reassesses the patient's oxygen saturation

3.2 Hypoglycaemia

Conscious patient with safe swallow

  • Oral glucose gel: commonly 10–20 g
  • Recheck glucose after approximately 10–15 minutes
  • Repeat according to local protocol
/me checks the patient's glucose and identifies symptomatic hypoglycaemia
/me confirms the patient is alert and has a safe swallow
/me opens a glucose gel packet and administers it orally
/me repeats the blood glucose reading after treatment

Patient unable to swallow

A commonly used adult hospital regimen is:

  • 10% glucose, 150–200 mL IV, administered carefully
  • Recheck glucose after 10–15 minutes
  • Avoid oral treatment if the airway is unsafe

The exact ambulance concentration and volume should be set by your server’s medicines policy.

/me confirms the patient cannot safely swallow
/me opens the IV glucose packaging and checks the solution
/me connects the glucose infusion to the patent cannula
/me administers IV glucose while monitoring the patient's airway and cardiac rhythm
/me repeats the capillary blood glucose after treatment

3.3 Aspirin

Suspected TIA

  • 300 mg orally immediately, if appropriate

Ischaemic stroke after haemorrhage excluded

  • 300 mg orally
  • If dysphagic: 300 mg rectally or via enteral tube in hospital

NICE recommends 300 mg daily initially, usually until definitive long-term antithrombotic treatment is started. [1] &#x20;

Do not administer if:

  • Intracranial haemorrhage is suspected or confirmed
  • Active significant bleeding
  • Severe aspirin allergy
  • The patient has already received an incompatible antithrombotic decision
  • A consultant has specifically withheld it
/me checks the CT report and confirms there is no intracranial haemorrhage
/me checks the patient's allergy and bleeding history
/me opens the aspirin packaging and prepares a 300 mg dose
/me administers the aspirin by the approved route
/me documents the dose, route and administration time

3.4 Alteplase

Adult acute ischaemic stroke dose

  • 0.9 mg/kg IV
  • Maximum 90 mg
  • 10% as an IV bolus over approximately 1 minute
  • Remaining 90% by infusion over 60 minutes

Example for a 70 kg patient:

  • Total: 63 mg
  • Bolus: 6.3 mg
  • Infusion: 56.7 mg over 60 minutes

Alteplase should only be given in an organised stroke service with appropriate imaging, monitoring and trained staff. [1] The MHRA product information is available here. [2] &#x20;

/me confirms the stroke consultant has approved alteplase
/me checks the patient's recorded weight and calculates 0.9 mg per kilogram
/me checks that the total dose does not exceed 90 mg
/me opens the alteplase packaging using aseptic technique
/me reconstitutes the medication according to the product instructions
/me performs a two-person check of the patient, dose, route and expiry date
/me administers 10 percent of the dose as an IV bolus
/me connects the remaining dose to an infusion pump for 60 minutes
/me records the door-to-needle time and starts post-thrombolysis observations

Example contraindication roleplay

  • CT shows haemorrhage
  • BP cannot be controlled below 185/110
  • Significant recent bleeding
  • Recent major surgery or head trauma
  • Known significant bleeding disorder
  • Significant anticoagulant effect
  • Symptoms have fully resolved
  • Consultant decides risk outweighs benefit

3.5 Tenecteplase

Adult acute ischaemic stroke dose

  • 0.25 mg/kg IV single bolus
  • Maximum 25 mg

This should be administered only under the stroke-team pathway. The current UK stroke guideline supports 0.25 mg/kg rather than the higher 0.4 mg/kg regimen. [3] Product information is available from the MHRA. [4] &#x20;

/me confirms the consultant has chosen tenecteplase rather than alteplase
/me checks the patient's weight and calculates 0.25 mg per kilogram
/me confirms that the dose does not exceed 25 mg
/me opens and reconstitutes the tenecteplase vial according to the product instructions
/me performs an independent medication check
/me administers the tenecteplase as a slow IV bolus
/me records the exact administration time and begins neurological monitoring

Do not give alteplase and tenecteplase together.

3.6 Blood-pressure treatment

Before thrombolysis

Target:

  • Below 185/110 mmHg

After thrombolysis:

  • Maintain below approximately 180/105 mmHg for the first 24 hours, according to local protocol. [1] &#x20;

Nicardipine

A commonly used adult IV regimen:

  • Start 3–5 mg/hour
  • Increase by 0.5–1 mg/hour every 15 minutes
  • Maximum 15 mg/hour
  • Titrate to the prescribed BP target

Labetalol

A commonly used adult IV regimen:

  • 10–20 mg IV slowly over 1–2 minutes
  • Repeat or give additional 20–80 mg doses at intervals according to response
  • Maximum cumulative bolus dose often 300 mg
  • Alternatively, infusion according to local critical-care protocol

These are hospital-only medications for the roleplay pathway.

/me confirms the prescribed blood-pressure target with the senior doctor
/me checks the patient's pulse, blood pressure and contraindications
/me opens the nicardipine ampoule and prepares the infusion pump
/me connects the infusion to the patient's IV line
/me starts the infusion at the prescribed rate
/me repeats the blood pressure at regular intervals and titrates only on medical instruction

Do not rapidly lower BP in an ordinary ischaemic stroke that is not undergoing thrombolysis and has no hypertensive emergency.

3.7 Intracerebral haemorrhage BP pathway

For a suitable patient:

  • Consider treatment when SBP is 150–220 mmHg
  • Aim for approximately 130–140 mmHg systolic
  • Avoid a fall of more than approximately 60 mmHg within one hour

NICE states that BP treatment should be individualised, particularly where the patient has a very low GCS, a large haematoma, structural cause or planned early surgery. [1] &#x20;

/me confirms the CT shows intracerebral haemorrhage
/me connects the patient to continuous blood-pressure monitoring
/me prepares the prescribed nicardipine infusion
/me starts the infusion through an infusion pump
/me reassesses the blood pressure and neurological status at regular intervals
/me informs the consultant if the patient becomes hypotensive or neurologically worse

3.8 Warfarin reversal

For serious intracranial bleeding:

  • Four-factor PCC: commonly 25–50 units/kg IV, depending on INR and product protocol
  • Vitamin K: 5 mg IV is a commonly used emergency dose
  • Check INR after treatment and repeat treatment only on specialist instruction

NICE recommends PCC plus IV vitamin K for warfarin-associated intracerebral haemorrhage. [1] &#x20;

/me confirms the patient is taking warfarin and requests an urgent INR
/me informs the consultant and pharmacist of the intracranial haemorrhage
/me calculates the PCC dose from the patient's weight and INR
/me checks the PCC vial, expiry date and prescribed dose
/me prepares the PCC and IV vitamin K
/me administers the prescribed reversal treatment
/me arranges repeat INR testing and continued neurological observations

3.9 Dabigatran reversal

  • Idarucizumab 5 g IV total
  • Usually given as two separate 2.5 g IV doses, one after the other

This is consultant/haematology-led treatment.

/me confirms recent dabigatran exposure and informs the consultant
/me opens the idarucizumab packs and checks both 2.5 gram doses
/me administers the first IV dose according to hospital policy
/me administers the second dose and documents the total 5 gram dose
/me monitors for ongoing bleeding, thrombosis and neurological deterioration

UK guidance identifies idarucizumab as the specific reversal agent for dabigatran. [5] &#x20;

3.10 Apixaban or rivaroxaban reversal

Andexanet alfa licensed regimens:

Low dose

  • 400 mg IV bolus
  • Then 4 mg/min infusion for 120 minutes

High dose

  • 800 mg IV bolus
  • Then 8 mg/min infusion for 120 minutes

Use depends on the drug, dose and time since the last dose. NICE notes that andexanet is not routinely recommended for intracranial haemorrhage outside specific specialist/research arrangements, so your server should make this a consultant/haematology decision. [6] &#x20;

/me confirms the patient has taken apixaban or rivaroxaban
/me checks the time and amount of the last dose
/me discusses the reversal option with the consultant and haematologist
/me prepares the prescribed andexanet alfa regimen
/me administers the bolus using the prescribed rate
/me starts the two-hour infusion through an infusion pump
/me monitors for recurrent bleeding, thrombosis and neurological deterioration

There is no specific licensed reversal agent for edoxaban; specialist advice is required. [5] &#x20;

3.11 Paracetamol

For headache or fever:

  • Adult oral/IV dose: 1 g
  • Minimum interval: 4 hours
  • Maximum: 4 g in 24 hours in a standard adult
  • Use a lower maximum in low body weight, liver disease, malnutrition or alcohol dependence
/me confirms the patient's allergy, weight and recent paracetamol intake
/me opens the prescribed paracetamol packaging
/me administers 1 gram by the approved route
/me reassesses the patient's pain and temperature

Avoid masking neurological deterioration with excessive sedation or unnecessary medication.

3.12 Ondansetron

For significant nausea or vomiting:

  • 4 mg IV or oral
  • May be repeated according to local policy
  • Consider QT prolongation and interaction risks
/me checks the patient's ECG and medication history for QT-prolonging drugs
/me opens the ondansetron ampoule and checks the dose
/me administers 4 mg slowly through the IV line
/me reassesses the patient's nausea, vomiting and neurological status

In suspected intracranial haemorrhage, vomiting should trigger continued neurological reassessment, not simply repeated antiemetic treatment.

3.13 Nimodipine — subarachnoid haemorrhage

Nimodipine is used in aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage to reduce delayed cerebral ischaemia risk.

A common regimen is:

  • 60 mg orally or enterally every 4 hours
  • Usually for 21 days, under the neurovascular team

If oral/enteral treatment is impossible, specialist IV regimens may be used in a critical-care environment.

/me confirms the neurovascular consultant has diagnosed aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage
/me checks the patient's blood pressure before administering nimodipine
/me administers 60 mg via the approved oral or enteral route
/me monitors the patient's blood pressure and neurological status

Do not give nimodipine as an informal ED medication for every headache or suspected stroke.

3.14 Statin therapy

For established ischaemic stroke secondary prevention:

  • Atorvastatin 20–80 mg orally once daily, once medically stable and able to swallow safely

Immediate initiation is not normally the priority during hyperacute reperfusion decision-making. NICE advises continuing existing statin therapy and starting appropriate secondary prevention when safe. [1] &#x20;

/me confirms the swallowing assessment has been completed
/me reviews the lipid history and current statin prescription
/me administers the prescribed atorvastatin dose
/me documents the medication in the discharge and secondary-prevention plan

  • New headache
  • Vomiting
  • Sudden neurological decline
  • New weakness

  • Seizure
  • Bleeding from IV or puncture sites

  • Groin-site bleeding
  • Limb perfusion
  • Haematoma
  • Sudden headache
  • BP instability
  • Reperfusion haemorrhage
  • Reduced consciousness

  • GCS fall
  • Pupil asymmetry
  • New seizure
  • Worsening headache
  • Repeated vomiting
  • New weakness
  • Bradycardia with hypertension
  • Irregular breathing

  • Reduced consciousness
  • Seizure
  • New focal deficit
  • Hydrocephalus
  • Worsening confusion
  • Delayed cerebral ischaemia/vasospasm

  • Severe headache
  • New seizure

6. Important server rule for realistic play

Use this distinction:

Pre-hospital team

Recognise, stabilise, document, pre-alert and transport.

Hospital emergency team

Assess, image, exclude haemorrhage and activate the correct pathway.

Stroke consultant

Authorise thrombolysis, thrombectomy referral, specialist BP treatment and reversal.

Neurosurgical/neurointerventional team

Perform thrombectomy, aneurysm treatment, decompressive procedures and selected haemorrhage interventions.

This prevents paramedics from diagnosing the stroke subtype or administering high-risk medication before imaging.

FiveM Stroke SOP — Field Response, Hospital Management, Rank Permissions & MDT

FiveM Stroke SOP

Field Response, Hospital Management, Rank Permissions, /me Actions and MDT Templates

> Roleplay use only: This is a fictional FiveM medical SOP based broadly on UK/NHS stroke pathways. It is not a real-world clinical protocol. Real treatment requires qualified clinicians, approved medicines, monitoring and local ambulance/hospital guidelines.

NICE recommends rapid FAST assessment, early glucose testing, minimal scene delay, hospital pre-alert, urgent CT/CTA and specialist stroke-unit care. Aspirin is given after intracranial haemorrhage has been excluded, while thrombolysis is restricted to an organised stroke service. [1]

1. Stroke overview

1.1 Ischaemic stroke

A clot blocks blood flow to part of the brain.

Typical findings

  • Face droop
  • One-sided weakness or numbness
  • Speech difficulty
  • Visual field loss
  • Neglect
  • Gaze deviation
  • Ataxia
  • Sudden confusion
  • Possible atrial fibrillation

Possible treatments

  • Thrombolysis
  • Mechanical thrombectomy
  • Aspirin after CT excludes haemorrhage
  • Stroke-unit monitoring
  • Rehabilitation

1.2 Intracerebral haemorrhage

A blood vessel ruptures inside the brain.

Typical findings

  • Sudden severe headache
  • Vomiting
  • Rapid neurological deterioration
  • Very high blood pressure
  • Reduced consciousness
  • Seizure
  • Focal weakness
  • Unequal pupils
  • GCS decline

Possible treatments

  • Blood-pressure management
  • Anticoagulant reversal
  • Neurosurgical review
  • Critical-care admission
  • Repeat CT imaging
  • Airway and intracranial-pressure management

1.3 Subarachnoid haemorrhage

Bleeding occurs around the brain, often from a ruptured aneurysm.

Typical findings

  • Thunderclap headache
  • Sudden collapse
  • Neck stiffness
  • Photophobia
  • Vomiting
  • Seizure
  • Reduced consciousness
  • Focal neurological deficit

1.4 TIA

A temporary neurological deficit caused by reduced cerebral blood flow, with symptoms resolving completely.

Typical findings

  • Temporary facial weakness
  • Temporary speech loss
  • Temporary arm or leg weakness
  • Temporary visual loss
  • Full or near-full resolution before assessment

A TIA still requires urgent specialist review. NICE recommends specialist assessment within 24 hours and advises against using ABCD2 alone to determine urgency. [1]

3. General clinical authority

Student Paramedic

May:

  • Take basic observations under supervision
  • Perform FAST under supervision
  • Check blood glucose
  • Prepare equipment
  • Assist with PPE and packaging
  • Assist with airway management
  • Assist with documentation
  • Assist with patient movement

May not independently:

  • Give aspirin
  • Give thrombolysis
  • Give IV antihypertensives
  • Make final transport decisions
  • Diagnose stroke

Paramedic

May:

  • Perform full ABCDE
  • Perform FAST
  • Check blood glucose
  • Perform ECG
  • Establish IV access
  • Give oxygen for hypoxia
  • Treat hypoglycaemia
  • Give approved basic medications
  • Maintain nil-by-mouth precautions
  • Pre-alert hospital
  • Transport rapidly

May not independently:

  • Give thrombolysis
  • Give anticoagulant reversal
  • Give aspirin to an undifferentiated stroke before imaging
  • Start hospital-level blood-pressure infusions

Advanced Paramedic

May additionally:

  • Lead complex stroke assessments
  • Perform advanced airway management
  • Manage seizures
  • Supervise junior ambulance staff
  • Authorise medications within the ambulance SOP
  • Liaise directly with the stroke physician
  • Make advanced destination decisions

Consultant Paramedic

May additionally:

  • Lead ambulance clinical operations
  • Supervise all ambulance grades
  • Manage complex or unstable patients
  • Review medication events
  • Coordinate with hospital consultants
  • Authorise advanced pre-hospital treatments within service policy
  • Lead major incidents

Consultant Paramedics should not independently administer thrombolysis unless they are specifically credentialed to work within an authorised mobile stroke unit.

HART Paramedic and Advanced Paramedic

HART personnel retain their normal clinical permissions but may additionally:

  • Operate in hazardous environments
  • Manage chemical, fire, structural or violence-related risks
  • Work with fire and police services
  • Use specialist PPE
  • Extract patients from difficult locations
  • Manage contamination or restricted access

HART status alone does not give authority to administer thrombolysis or hospital-only medication.

  • Communicate with ground services

6. Field response algorithm

Stage 1 — Scene safety and PPE

/me checks the scene is safe for the crew and patient
/me identifies any hazards, witnesses and possible evidence of trauma or overdose
/me opens the PPE pouch and puts on gloves and eye protection
/me asks police or fire personnel to control any ongoing hazards

Look for

  • Road traffic collision
  • Assault
  • Fall or head injury
  • Drug use
  • Carbon monoxide or chemical exposure
  • Seizure-related injury
  • Unsafe crowd or aggressive behaviour

Stage 2 — Initial impression

/me approaches the patient and confirms they are responsive
/me introduces themselves and explains that they are going to assess the patient
/me observes the patient's facial symmetry, speech, breathing and limb position
/me asks the patient to state their name and location

Record:

  • Age
  • Level of consciousness
  • General appearance
  • Obvious neurological deficit
  • Respiratory effort
  • Presence of vomiting or seizure activity

Stage 3 — ABCDE assessment

A — Airway

/me asks the patient to open their mouth and speak
/me checks for airway obstruction, secretions, vomit and abnormal airway sounds
/me suctions visible secretions from the patient's mouth
/me positions the patient's head to maintain airway patency
/me prepares an airway adjunct because the patient's consciousness is reduced

Look for:

  • Snoring
  • Gurgling
  • Stridor
  • Drooling
  • Vomit
  • Weak cough
  • Inability to speak
  • Reduced consciousness
  • Loss of gag or airway protection

B — Breathing

/me observes the patient's chest rise and counts the respiratory rate
/me places a pulse-oximeter probe on the patient's finger
/me listens to both sides of the chest with a stethoscope
/me applies oxygen because the patient's oxygen saturation is low
/me reassesses the patient's breathing after oxygen is applied

Look for:

  • Hypoxia
  • Aspiration
  • Irregular breathing
  • Pulmonary oedema
  • Respiratory fatigue
  • Reduced respiratory drive
  • Vomit or secretions in the airway

Do not give routine oxygen to a patient with normal oxygen saturation. [1]

C — Circulation

/me checks the patient's radial pulse and skin colour
/me applies the blood-pressure cuff and obtains a reading
/me attaches the patient to the cardiac monitor
/me obtains a 12-lead ECG and checks the rhythm
/me establishes IV access while monitoring the patient

Look for:

  • Atrial fibrillation
  • Bradycardia or tachycardia
  • Shock
  • Severe hypertension
  • Chest pain
  • Pulmonary oedema
  • Aortic dissection symptoms

Do not automatically lower blood pressure in suspected ischaemic stroke. NICE recommends treatment mainly for specific emergencies or when the patient is being prepared for thrombolysis. [1]

D — Disability

/me assesses the patient's level of consciousness using AVPU
/me checks the patient's pupils for size and reaction
/me checks the patient's blood glucose
/me asks the patient to smile and observes for facial asymmetry
/me asks the patient to raise both arms and observes for drift
/me asks the patient to repeat a sentence and assesses their speech
/me checks limb strength, sensation, coordination and visual fields
/me records the initial neurological findings and GCS

Look for:

  • Facial droop
  • Arm or leg weakness
  • Aphasia
  • Dysarthria
  • Neglect
  • Gaze deviation
  • Visual field loss
  • Ataxia
  • Seizure
  • Reduced consciousness
  • Unequal pupils
  • Worsening neurological signs

E — Exposure

/me checks the patient for signs of trauma, rash, bleeding and medical alert bracelets
/me checks for evidence of seizure activity or incontinence
/me checks the patient's medication packets and identification
/me covers the patient with a blanket and maintains their dignity

Look for:

  • Head injury
  • Neck injury
  • Signs of anticoagulant use
  • Infection or fever
  • Drug paraphernalia
  • Recent surgery
  • Evidence of bleeding

7. Stroke assessment

FAST

/me asks the patient to smile and checks for facial drooping
/me asks the patient to raise both arms and checks for weakness or drift
/me asks the patient to repeat a sentence and checks for speech disturbance
/me confirms the exact time the symptoms began or when the patient was last known well

Additional questions

/me asks the witness when the patient was last seen completely normal
/me asks whether the symptoms began suddenly or developed gradually
/me asks about seizures, collapse, headache or recent trauma
/me asks about previous stroke, atrial fibrillation, diabetes and hypertension
/me asks whether the patient takes warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban or dabigatran
/me asks the family about the patient's normal mobility and independence

Nil-by-mouth action

/me explains that the patient must not eat, drink or take tablets until their swallowing is assessed
/me removes food and drink from within the patient's reach
/me positions the patient safely and keeps suction available

NICE recommends a swallowing screen before food, fluid or oral medication is given. [1]

8. Field observations by stroke type

Likely ischaemic stroke

Observe for:

  • Sudden unilateral weakness
  • Facial droop
  • Aphasia or dysarthria
  • Visual field loss
  • Neglect
  • Gaze deviation
  • Atrial fibrillation
  • Usually preserved consciousness initially
  • Symptoms that may fluctuate

Key documentation:

  • Last-known-well
  • FAST result
  • NIHSS if trained
  • Blood glucose
  • ECG rhythm
  • Anticoagulant use
  • Baseline independence

Likely intracerebral haemorrhage

Observe for:

  • Severe headache
  • Vomiting
  • Rapid decline in GCS
  • Seizure
  • Very high blood pressure
  • Unequal pupils
  • Worsening weakness
  • Reduced airway protection
  • Cushing-type features in severe deterioration

Actions:

  • Frequent neurological reassessment
  • Airway preparation
  • Rapid transport
  • No aspirin
  • Immediate hospital pre-alert

Likely subarachnoid haemorrhage

Observe for:

  • Thunderclap headache
  • Neck stiffness
  • Photophobia
  • Vomiting
  • Collapse
  • Seizures
  • Reduced consciousness
  • Sudden neurological deficit

Actions:

  • Treat as a time-critical neurological emergency
  • Avoid oral intake
  • Monitor airway and consciousness
  • Pre-alert for urgent CT and specialist review

TIA

Observe for:

  • Symptoms completely resolved
  • No current weakness or speech deficit
  • Normal or near-normal FAST at assessment
  • Previous transient episodes
  • Ongoing risk factors such as atrial fibrillation or carotid disease

Actions:

  • Record the original symptoms carefully
  • Record their duration
  • Record exact resolution time
  • Refer urgently
  • Give aspirin only according to the server TIA protocol and after checking contraindications

9. Stroke mimics

Consider:

  • Hypoglycaemia
  • Seizure with postictal weakness
  • Migraine with aura
  • Intoxication
  • Sepsis or delirium
  • Brain tumour
  • Bell’s palsy
  • Syncope
  • Functional neurological disorder
  • Subdural haematoma
  • Severe electrolyte disturbance
/me checks the patient's blood glucose to exclude hypoglycaemia as a stroke mimic
/me asks witnesses whether the patient had a seizure or abnormal movements
/me checks for recent head trauma or intoxication
/me reassesses the neurological examination for consistency and progression

A negative FAST does not completely exclude stroke. Persistent focal symptoms should still be transported and assessed as possible stroke. [2]

11. Hospital stroke pathway

Resuscitation/stroke bay

/me receives the patient onto the stroke trolley and connects them to cardiac and oxygen-saturation monitoring
/me confirms the last-known-well time with the ambulance crew
/me repeats the ABCDE assessment
/me checks the patient's glucose and obtains a full set of observations
/me confirms the patient's anticoagulant and antiplatelet history

Doctor assessment

/me performs a focused neurological assessment and calculates the NIHSS
/me asks the family about the patient's baseline function and medical history
/me checks for recent surgery, trauma, bleeding or anticoagulant use
/me requests urgent CT brain and CT angiography
/me informs the stroke consultant that the patient is ready for imaging

Imaging

/me transfers the patient onto the CT table while maintaining monitoring
/me confirms the patient's identity and last-known-well time with radiology staff
/me performs a non-contrast CT brain
/me performs CT angiography to assess for a large-vessel occlusion
/me transfers the images to the stroke consultant and thrombectomy team

NICE recommends immediate CT for potential thrombolysis/thrombectomy candidates, patients taking anticoagulants, patients with reduced consciousness or severe headache, and CTA when thrombectomy may be indicated. [1]

12. Imaging-based treatment decisions

CT shows haemorrhage

/me reviews the CT and confirms intracranial haemorrhage
/me withholds aspirin, anticoagulants and thrombolytic medication
/me connects the patient to continuous blood-pressure monitoring
/me contacts the stroke consultant, neurosurgeon and critical-care team
/me reviews the medication history for anticoagulant use

Potential management:

  • Blood-pressure management
  • PCC and vitamin K for warfarin-related bleeding
  • Idarucizumab for dabigatran where appropriate
  • Specialist reversal for factor-Xa inhibitors
  • Neurosurgical referral
  • ICU or hyperacute stroke-unit admission

NICE recommends urgent reversal of warfarin-associated intracerebral haemorrhage with PCC and IV vitamin K. [1]

CT shows no haemorrhage

/me confirms that the CT does not show intracranial haemorrhage
/me reviews the patient's treatment time window and contraindications
/me discusses thrombolysis eligibility with the stroke consultant
/me reviews the CTA for a proximal large-vessel occlusion

Possible actions:

  • Thrombolysis
  • Thrombectomy referral
  • Aspirin after the treatment decision
  • Stroke-unit admission
  • Supportive treatment

CTA shows a large-vessel occlusion

/me confirms a proximal vessel occlusion on CT angiography
/me informs the stroke consultant and neurointerventional team
/me prepares the patient for urgent thrombectomy referral
/me confirms the last-known-well time and baseline functional status
/me arranges transfer to the thrombectomy centre if required

NICE recommends thrombectomy as soon as possible and, in selected patients, up to 24 hours from last-known-well when imaging shows potentially salvageable brain tissue. [1]

Patient details

  • Patient: John Smith
  • Age: 68
  • Last-known-well: 13:40
  • Found symptomatic: 14:05
  • Arrival: 14:32
  • Baseline: Independent, walks unaided

Presentation

  • Left facial droop
  • Left arm and leg weakness
  • Expressive aphasia
  • Rightward gaze deviation
  • FAST positive
  • NIHSS: 16
  • GCS: 14

Observations

  • BP: 178/102
  • HR: 118
  • Rhythm: Atrial fibrillation
  • SpO₂: 97% on air
  • Glucose: 6.8 mmol/L
  • Temperature: 36.8°C
  • Airway: Patent
  • Swallow: Not yet assessed; nil by mouth

Relevant history

  • Previous hypertension
  • Atrial fibrillation
  • Apixaban reported by family
  • Last apixaban dose unknown
  • No known allergies
  • No recent surgery
  • No trauma

  • CT: No intracranial haemorrhage
  • CTA: Right proximal MCA occlusion
  • CT perfusion: Salvageable tissue present
  • Radiologist: Confirms large-vessel occlusion

MDT discussion

Ambulance clinician:

> “Symptoms began at approximately 13:40. Patient was independent at baseline. No trauma. Family reports apixaban, last dose unknown.”

> “Airway is currently protected. GCS 14. NIHSS 16. Blood pressure is below the local thrombolysis threshold but anticoagulant status requires clarification.”

19. Core realism rules

  1. Time is critical: document last-known-well accurately.
  2. Check glucose early: hypoglycaemia can mimic stroke.
  3. Do not give aspirin to an undifferentiated stroke before imaging.
  4. Do not thrombolyse before CT excludes haemorrhage.
  5. Do not routinely lower blood pressure in ischaemic stroke.
  6. Keep patients nil by mouth until swallowing is assessed.
  7. Paramedics identify and transport; hospital stroke teams diagnose and reperfuse.
  8. Thrombolysis requires senior authorisation and monitoring.
  9. Thrombectomy requires CTA and specialist referral.
  10. Any neurological deterioration requires immediate ABCDE reassessment and senior escalation.

FiveM Stroke Guide — UK/NHS-Informed Roleplay Protocol

FiveM Stroke Guide — UK/NHS-Informed Roleplay Protocol

> Important: This is designed for FiveM roleplay, not real-life treatment. Real stroke care must follow current JRCALC, local ambulance trust, hospital and specialist-stroke protocols. Do not use these doses or procedures on real patients without appropriate training, authority and monitoring.

The roleplay model below assumes an NHS-style system with:

  • Ambulance/paramedic service
  • Emergency department
  • Hyperacute stroke unit
  • CT/CTA imaging
  • Thrombolysis and thrombectomy capability
  • Neurosurgery and rehabilitation services

NICE recommends rapid stroke recognition, exclusion of hypoglycaemia, urgent imaging, direct admission to a specialist stroke service and treatment based on whether the stroke is ischaemic or haemorrhagic. [1]

1. What is a stroke?

A stroke is sudden neurological dysfunction caused by either:

Ischaemic stroke

A blood vessel is blocked by a clot or atherosclerotic disease.

Common mechanisms:

  • Large-vessel occlusion — internal carotid, middle cerebral or basilar artery
  • Cardioembolic stroke — often associated with atrial fibrillation
  • Small-vessel/lacunar stroke
  • Arterial dissection
  • Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis

Potential treatments:

  • Intravenous thrombolysis
  • Mechanical thrombectomy
  • Antiplatelet therapy
  • Stroke-unit monitoring and rehabilitation

Haemorrhagic stroke

A blood vessel ruptures and bleeds into or around the brain.

Types:

  • Intracerebral haemorrhage — bleeding into brain tissue
  • Subarachnoid haemorrhage — bleeding around the brain
  • Intraventricular haemorrhage — bleeding into the ventricles

Potential treatments:

  • Blood-pressure control
  • Reversal of anticoagulants
  • Neurosurgical assessment
  • External ventricular drain or haematoma surgery in selected cases
  • Intensive neurological monitoring

You cannot reliably distinguish an ischaemic stroke from a haemorrhage clinically. A CT scan is required.

2. Stroke recognition

FAST

| Letter | Assessment | | -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | | F — Face | Ask the patient to smile. Is one side drooping? | | A — Arms | Ask them to raise both arms. Does one drift or fall? | | S — Speech | Ask them to repeat a sentence. Is speech slurred, confused or absent? | | T — Time | Establish the exact onset or last-known-well time and call an emergency response. |

Additional warning signs

  • Sudden unilateral weakness or numbness
  • Sudden visual loss or double vision
  • Sudden severe imbalance or ataxia
  • Sudden confusion
  • Sudden difficulty understanding speech
  • Sudden severe headache
  • Collapse or reduced consciousness
  • New seizure
  • Neck pain with neurological symptoms, suggesting arterial dissection

A patient who is FAST-negative may still have a posterior circulation or subtle stroke. Persistent focal neurological symptoms should be treated seriously. [2]

6. On-scene paramedic assessment

Step 1: Scene safety

Check:

  • Traffic, violence, fire or hazardous substances
  • Possible overdose
  • Evidence of trauma or assault
  • Witness availability
  • Last-known-well information
  • Whether police are required

Do not assume that a collapsed patient has had a stroke. Trauma, seizure, hypoglycaemia, intoxication and sepsis can mimic stroke.

Step 2: Primary survey — ABCDE

A — Airway

Assess:

  • Is the patient speaking?
  • Is there snoring, gurgling or obstruction?
  • Are secretions pooling?
  • Is there vomiting?
  • Is the gag reflex impaired?

Management:

  • Position safely
  • Suction secretions
  • Use airway adjuncts if required
  • Consider recovery position if unconscious but breathing
  • Avoid unnecessary intubation in a stable patient
  • Escalate early if airway protection is failing

B — Breathing

Assess:

  • Respiratory rate
  • Work of breathing
  • Oxygen saturation
  • Chest movement
  • Aspiration or pulmonary oedema

Give oxygen if hypoxic. Routine oxygen in a patient with normal oxygen saturation is not recommended in acute stroke. NICE uses a target approach where oxygen is given when saturation is below 95%. [1]

C — Circulation

Assess:

  • Pulse and rhythm
  • Blood pressure in both arms if appropriate
  • Capillary refill
  • Skin temperature
  • ECG for atrial fibrillation or arrhythmia
  • Signs of shock

Do not routinely lower blood pressure in the ambulance for suspected ischaemic stroke. A high blood pressure may be compensatory. Exceptions include a hypertensive emergency, aortic dissection, pulmonary oedema or a specialist-directed pathway.

D — Disability

Perform:

  • AVPU or GCS
  • FAST
  • Blood glucose
  • Pupils
  • Limb power and sensation
  • Speech and language assessment
  • Seizure assessment
  • NIHSS if trained and time permits

E — Exposure

Look for:

  • Trauma
  • Needle marks
  • Medic-alert bracelets
  • Medication packets
  • Rash or infection
  • Fever
  • Incontinence
  • Evidence of seizure

Maintain dignity and avoid unnecessary scene delay.

  • Check blood glucose early

  • Notify the receiving hospital

  • Do not give food, drink or oral medication.

  • Do not delay transport for a lengthy examination.
  • Do not give anticoagulants.
  • Do not assume improvement means the event was harmless.
  • Do not allow the patient to walk unaided.
  • Do not use steroids or mannitol routinely for ordinary stroke.

Immediate actions

  1. Receive pre-alert.
  2. Move directly to resuscitation or stroke assessment area.
  3. Confirm last-known-well.
  4. Repeat ABCDE and glucose.
  5. Establish neurological baseline.
  6. Perform ROSIER or local stroke assessment.
  7. Obtain NIHSS.
  8. Review medications and contraindications.
  9. Obtain urgent blood tests.
  10. Send for CT brain and CTA where appropriate.
  11. Contact stroke physician.
  12. Contact thrombectomy centre if large-vessel occlusion is suspected.

11. NIHSS roleplay assessment

The NIHSS gives a structured estimate of stroke severity.

Assess:

  1. Level of consciousness
  2. Orientation questions
  3. Commands
  4. Gaze
  5. Visual fields
  6. Facial weakness
  7. Motor arm
  8. Motor leg
  9. Limb ataxia
  10. Sensory loss
  11. Language

Roleplay interpretation

13. Hospital treatment: ischaemic stroke

A. Thrombolysis

In a suitable adult with acute ischaemic stroke, no intracranial haemorrhage on imaging and a suitable treatment window, thrombolysis may be considered by the stroke team.

NICE supports intravenous thrombolysis within 4.5 hours where appropriate and states that alteplase or tenecteplase should only be used within a properly organised stroke service with imaging, trained staff and monitoring capability. [1]

Roleplay alteplase dose

Alteplase: 0.9 mg/kg IV, maximum 90 mg

  • 10% of total dose as an IV bolus
  • Remaining 90% infused over 60 minutes

Example:

  • 70 kg patient
  • Total dose: 63 mg
  • Bolus: 6.3 mg
  • Infusion: 56.7 mg over 60 minutes

Roleplay tenecteplase dose

A common stroke-service regimen is:

Tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg IV bolus, maximum 25 mg

This should be shown in roleplay as a single specialist-administered IV bolus after CT/CTA and stroke-physician authorisation.

Do not thrombolyse in roleplay if there is

  • Intracranial haemorrhage
  • Uncontrolled severe hypertension
  • Active significant bleeding
  • Recent major surgery or serious trauma
  • Known bleeding disorder
  • Significant anticoagulant effect
  • Previous intracranial haemorrhage where contraindicated
  • Suspected aortic dissection
  • Symptoms that have fully resolved, unless a specialist confirms a disabling deficit or another indication
  • Unclear or unsuitable timing without specialist review

The exact eligibility criteria should be implemented through a server-specific stroke consultant decision rather than an automatic paramedic action.

Thrombolysis monitoring

After administration:

  • Admit to hyperacute stroke unit or equivalent
  • Frequent neurological observations
  • Frequent blood-pressure checks
  • Watch for severe headache
  • Watch for vomiting or acute neurological deterioration
  • Watch for bleeding
  • Avoid unnecessary invasive procedures
  • Repeat brain imaging if deterioration occurs
  • Do not give antiplatelets or anticoagulants immediately without specialist direction

Suspected thrombolysis complication

If the patient deteriorates:

  1. Stop the infusion if still running.
  2. Call the stroke physician and senior emergency clinician.
  3. Reassess ABCDE.
  4. Urgent CT brain.
  5. Check FBC and coagulation studies.
  6. Prepare blood products or haemostatic treatment according to specialist protocol.
  7. Notify neurosurgery and critical care.

14. Mechanical thrombectomy

Thrombectomy is a catheter-based procedure in which a clot is removed from a large cerebral artery.

Roleplay eligibility

Consider when there is:

  • Clinical diagnosis of acute ischaemic stroke
  • Significant neurological deficit
  • Proximal large-vessel occlusion on CTA/MRA
  • Small enough established infarct core
  • Acceptable baseline function
  • Suitable treatment window

NICE recommends thrombectomy as soon as possible and generally within 6 hours for selected proximal anterior-circulation occlusions. Selected patients may be treated between 6 and 24 hours when imaging shows potentially salvageable brain tissue. [1]

Typical workflow

  1. CT brain
  2. CTA
  3. CT perfusion if late presentation
  4. Stroke consultant decision
  5. Neurointerventional referral
  6. Transfer to thrombectomy centre if required
  7. Anaesthetic assessment
  8. Femoral or radial arterial access
  9. Catheter navigation to cerebral circulation
  10. Stent retriever and/or aspiration thrombectomy
  11. Reperfusion assessment
  12. Intensive or stroke-unit monitoring

Pre-hospital considerations

  • Do not delay transport for scene-based thrombectomy decisions.
  • Follow local bypass policy.
  • Notify the receiving centre early if large-vessel occlusion is suspected.
  • Record the last-known-well and baseline function accurately.

15. Hospital treatment: intracerebral haemorrhage

Immediate management

  • ABCDE and airway protection
  • Hyperacute stroke-unit or critical-care admission
  • Urgent CT brain
  • CTA where indicated
  • Neurological observations
  • Blood-pressure management
  • Stop antiplatelet and anticoagulant medications
  • Urgent reversal of anticoagulation if relevant
  • Neurosurgical consultation
  • Repeat CT if neurological status worsens

Blood pressure

NICE recommends considering rapid blood-pressure reduction in suitable patients with intracerebral haemorrhage, particularly when:

  • Presentation is within 6 hours
  • Systolic BP is 150–220 mmHg

The target is generally around 140 mmHg or lower, while avoiding a fall of more than 60 mmHg in the first hour. [1]

For roleplay, the medication should be administered by the ED/critical-care team using a locally agreed infusion pathway, for example:

  • Nicardipine infusion
  • Labetalol boluses or infusion
  • Other locally approved IV antihypertensive

Do not make paramedic crews independently lower blood pressure in an undifferentiated stroke unless the server’s protocol specifically authorises it.

Anticoagulant reversal

Warfarin

  • Urgent INR measurement
  • Four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate
  • Intravenous vitamin K

Dabigatran

  • Idarucizumab may be used in hospital

Factor Xa inhibitors

  • Specialist-directed reversal
  • Four-factor PCC may be considered
  • Andexanet alfa may be considered in appropriate circumstances

NICE recommends rapid reversal of warfarin-associated intracerebral haemorrhage using PCC and IV vitamin K. [1]

  • Sudden “thunderclap” headache
  • Collapse
  • Neck stiffness
  • Vomiting
  • Photophobia
  • Reduced consciousness
  • Seizure
  • Focal neurological deficit

Management

  • ABCDE
  • Analgesia and antiemetic treatment
  • Avoid unnecessary hypertension
  • Urgent CT brain and CTA
  • Neurosurgical/neurovascular referral
  • Critical-care admission
  • Monitor for rebleeding, hydrocephalus and vasospasm

Do not give aspirin or anticoagulants until haemorrhage has been excluded.

18. TIA pathway

TIA symptoms resolve completely, but the risk of a subsequent stroke can be significant.

Symptoms

  • Temporary weakness
  • Temporary speech disturbance
  • Temporary visual loss
  • Temporary facial droop
  • Symptoms that have now resolved

Roleplay management

  1. FAST/neurological assessment
  2. Blood glucose
  3. Determine symptom duration and last-known-well
  4. Check for ongoing deficit
  5. If symptoms are completely resolved, treat as suspected TIA
  6. Aspirin 300 mg may be given if no contraindication and haemorrhage is not suspected
  7. Urgent specialist assessment
  8. Carotid imaging and MRI where appropriate
  9. Do not use ABCD2 alone to decide urgency

NICE recommends immediate aspirin 300 mg for suspected TIA unless contraindicated and specialist assessment within 24 hours. [1]

Scene

  1. Emergency call: “Patient suddenly cannot move right side.”
  2. Dispatcher asks for last-known-well.
  3. Police secure the scene if needed.
  4. EMT performs FAST and glucose.
  5. Paramedic completes ABCDE and ECG.
  6. Patient kept nil by mouth.
  7. Stroke pre-alert sent.
  8. Patient transported rapidly.

ED

  1. Resus/stroke bay activated.
  2. NIHSS completed.
  3. Bloods and IV access.
  4. CT brain and CTA performed.
  5. Result classified:

CT shows no bleed, vessel occlusion present

  • Stroke consultant activated
  • Thrombolysis considered
  • Thrombectomy referral made

CT shows no bleed, no large-vessel occlusion

  • Stroke-unit admission
  • Aspirin 300 mg
  • Monitoring and supportive treatment

CT shows intracerebral haemorrhage

  • No aspirin or thrombolysis
  • BP treatment
  • Anticoagulant reversal
  • Neurosurgical referral
  • Stroke-unit or ICU admission

Symptoms resolved

  • Suspected TIA pathway
  • Aspirin 300 mg if appropriate
  • Urgent specialist review

24. Server balancing suggestions

For fair and realistic FiveM gameplay:

Paramedics should be able to

  • Recognise stroke
  • Perform FAST and glucose
  • Provide oxygen/airway care
  • Establish monitoring and IV access
  • Pre-alert and transport
  • Perform basic stroke documentation

Paramedics should not automatically be able to

  • Diagnose ischaemic versus haemorrhagic stroke
  • Administer thrombolysis independently
  • Give aspirin before imaging
  • Perform thrombectomy
  • Reverse anticoagulation without medical authorisation
  • Rapidly lower every high blood pressure reading

Hospital doctors should be able to

  • Activate stroke pathway
  • Request CT/CTA
  • Calculate NIHSS
  • Authorise thrombolysis
  • Request thrombectomy
  • Manage BP and anticoagulant reversal
  • Admit to stroke unit or ICU

Suggested game timers

  • Dispatch to arrival: 5–10 minutes
  • Stroke assessment: 2–4 minutes
  • Pre-alert: immediately after positive screen
  • CT availability: 5–10 minutes
  • CT result: 2–5 minutes
  • Stroke consultant decision: 2–5 minutes
  • Thrombolysis: 1–3 minutes after decision
  • Thrombectomy transfer: server-dependent

This maintains urgency without making every stroke an instant treatment or guaranteed recovery.

Use speed as a mechanism clue, not a way to predict an exact BP or injury pattern.

Low speed~5–20 mph

Consider soft-tissue injury, minor head injury, fractures or occupant contact. Still assess properly.

Moderate speed~20–40 mph

Higher concern for fractures, chest/abdominal injury and head injury depending on impact.

High speed~40–60+ mph

Major trauma mechanism. Consider multiple injuries, significant haemorrhage, chest injury and traumatic brain injury.

Rollover / ejectionAny speed

High-risk mechanism. Look for multiple injuries and consider spinal injury according to the scenario.

What to look for

  • Scene safety, traffic and fuel/fire hazards
  • Catastrophic external bleeding
  • Airway and breathing difficulty
  • Chest wall injury, unequal movement or severe respiratory distress
  • Abdominal/pelvic pain or tenderness
  • Head injury, confusion, reduced consciousness
  • Fractures, deformity and distal circulation/sensation
  • Signs of shock: pale/cold/clammy skin, weakness, dizziness, rapid breathing

BP & observations

There is no reliable BP assigned to a crash speed. A patient with major blood loss may develop hypotension, but early shock can exist before BP falls.

Record a full set of baseline observations: HR, BP, RR, SpO₂, GCS, temperature, capillary refill, pain score. Repeat observations after every intervention and at least every 10 minutes in significant trauma. Watch for trends suggesting deterioration rather than relying on single readings.

Immediate priorities

  1. Make the scene safe and call appropriate resources.
  2. Use <C>ABCDE for suspected major trauma.
  3. Control life-threatening haemorrhage — direct pressure, haemostatic gauze or tourniquet.
  4. Assess airway/breathing and manage immediately life-threatening chest problems (tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, flail segment).
  5. Assess circulation, neurological status and expose for systematic head-to-toe assessment.
  6. Arrange appropriate trauma-centre escalation/transport in the RP scenario.

Pain management

Mild (1-3/10): Paracetamol 1g oral or ibuprofen 400mg. Moderate (4-6/10): Oral morphine 10-20mg or codeine 30-60mg. Severe (7-10/10): IV morphine 2.5-10mg titrated, or IV fentanyl 25-50mcg. Reassess pain score 15 min after each dose. Consider Entonox for procedural analgesia during splinting or wound care.

Transport & handover

SBAR handover to receiving unit:

  1. Situation — RTC, mechanism, speed, occupant position, seatbelt use
  2. Background — medical history, medications, allergies, tetanus status
  3. Assessment — injuries found, observations (HR/BP/RR/SpO₂/GCS/pain), treatment given, response
  4. Recommendation — trauma team assessment, imaging, surgical opinion for fractures or internal injury

Transport to a trauma unit with appropriate surgical capabilities.

RP treatment flow

Bleeding → haemorrhage control. Airway/breathing problem → oxygen/airway support. Suspected fracture → splint after appropriate assessment and recheck neurovascular status. Head injury → repeated neurological observations. Suspected internal injury → urgent escalation. Document all findings, treatment and observations for handover.

Vehicle Explosion / Fire

Think blast + burns + smoke inhalation + trauma, not simply “burns”.

Look for

  • Flame/thermal burns
  • Blast-related blunt or penetrating injuries
  • Facial burns, soot around mouth/nose
  • Hoarse voice, cough, wheeze or breathing difficulty
  • Confusion, collapse or reduced consciousness
  • Ear pain/hearing changes
  • Eye injury
  • Hidden chest/abdominal injuries
  • Fractures from blast wave or debris strike

Immediate priorities

  1. Do not enter an unsafe scene.
  2. Remove the patient from ongoing danger when safe and appropriate.
  3. Use <C>ABCDE and identify major haemorrhage.
  4. Prioritise airway/breathing when facial burns or inhalation injury are suspected.
  5. Cool significant thermal burns with cool running water where appropriate; protect from hypothermia.
  6. Cover burns appropriately and escalate serious burns.

BP & shock

Explosion severity does not determine an exact BP. Major bleeding, burns and physiological stress can change observations. Treat the patient's presentation and trends rather than inventing a speed/BP formula.

Hospital RP

Expect trauma assessment, repeated observations, wound/burn assessment, analgesia, blood tests/imaging as indicated, respiratory assessment and specialist burn/trauma referral where appropriate.

Gunshot Wounds

Location changes the likely complications. Treat the patient, not the weapon story.

Head / neck

Airway risk, neurological injury and major bleeding. Rapid <C>ABCDE, bleeding control and urgent trauma escalation.

Chest

Look for respiratory distress, penetrating chest wound and shock. Urgent assessment for life-threatening chest trauma.

Abdomen

Do not assume a small external wound means a small injury. Look for pain, guarding, shock and internal haemorrhage; urgent trauma assessment.

Pelvis / groin

Potential major haemorrhage. Assess circulation and treat life-threatening bleeding while escalating rapidly.

Arm

Control external bleeding. Check distal pulse, sensation and movement before/after dressing or immobilisation.

Leg

Control major bleeding, assess distal circulation/sensation/movement and immobilise associated fractures where appropriate.

Hand / foot

Bleeding and structural injury may coexist. Check distal function and protect the wound for definitive assessment.

Multiple wounds

Search for entry/exit wounds and consider multiple injured body regions. Prioritise life threats before detailed wound care.

RP rule: Never assign a BP solely from "gunshot severity". A low BP can be a late/serious sign of shock, while a patient can initially have a relatively preserved BP despite major injury.

Immediate priorities

  1. Confirm the scene is safe and any weapon threat is controlled before approaching.
  2. Use <C>ABCDE and control catastrophic haemorrhage first.
  3. Identify all entry and exit wounds — mark locations and do not probe the wound tract.
  4. Assess airway and breathing, especially if torso, neck or head is involved.
  5. Assess circulation and signs of shock — do not assume a normal BP rules out haemorrhage.
  6. Cannulate with large-bore IV access and prepare for fluid resuscitation if shocked.
  7. Record full observations and reassess frequently.

Pain management

Mild (1-3/10): Paracetamol 1g oral or ibuprofen 400mg. Moderate (4-6/10): Oral morphine 10-20mg or codeine 30-60mg. Severe (7-10/10): IV morphine 2.5-10mg titrated, or IV fentanyl 25-50mcg. Consider Entonox for procedural analgesia.

Transport & handover

SBAR handover:

  1. Situation — gunshot wound(s), location(s), time since injury
  2. Background — medical history, anticoagulants, allergies, tetanus status
  3. Assessment — entry/exit wounds, estimated blood loss, observations (HR/BP/RR/SpO2/GCS/pain), treatment given, response
  4. Recommendation — trauma team activation, wound exploration, imaging, surgical opinion

Hospital RP

Expect trauma team assessment, wound exploration, imaging, surgical wound management, IV antibiotics, tetanus booster if indicated, and ongoing vital sign monitoring.

Penetrating / Stab Wounds

Assume potentially serious internal injury until assessed. Do not probe or remove an embedded object.

Neck

Airway and major-vessel risk. Control external bleeding carefully and escalate immediately.

Chest

Watch for severe breathlessness, chest pain, reduced air entry and shock. Treat life threats and escalate for definitive chest-trauma management.

Abdomen

Potential internal bleeding or organ injury. Cover/protect wounds, assess repeatedly and arrange urgent trauma care.

Back / flank

Consider hidden chest, abdominal or spinal injury depending on location and mechanism.

Arm / forearm

Control bleeding and check distal circulation, sensation and movement. Consider tendon/nerve/vessel injury.

Thigh / groin

Potential major haemorrhage. Immediate haemorrhage control and urgent escalation.

Embedded object

Do not pull it out. Stabilise it where appropriate and arrange urgent definitive care.

Multiple wounds

Search systematically for additional injuries and entry/exit wounds while maintaining priorities.

Immediate priorities

  1. Confirm the scene is safe and any weapon threat is controlled before approaching.
  2. Use <C>ABCDE and do not remove any embedded object — stabilise it in place.
  3. Control external bleeding with direct pressure — apply occlusive dressing to any penetrating chest wound.
  4. Assess airway and breathing, especially if neck or chest is involved.
  5. Assess circulation and signs of shock — cannulate with large-bore IV access.
  6. Record full observations and reassess frequently.

Pain management

Mild (1-3/10): Paracetamol 1g oral or ibuprofen 400mg. Moderate (4-6/10): Oral morphine 10-20mg or codeine 30-60mg. Severe (7-10/10): IV morphine 2.5-10mg titrated, or IV fentanyl 25-50mcg. Consider Entonox for procedural analgesia.

Transport & handover

SBAR handover:

  1. Situation — penetrating injury, mechanism, wound location(s), time since injury
  2. Background — medical history, anticoagulants, allergies, tetanus status
  3. Assessment — wound(s), estimated blood loss, observations (HR/BP/RR/SpO2/GCS/pain), treatment given, response
  4. Recommendation — trauma team, wound exploration, imaging, surgical opinion

Hospital RP

Expect trauma team assessment, wound exploration, imaging, surgical wound management, IV antibiotics, tetanus booster if indicated, and ongoing monitoring.

Burns

Classify by depth, size, location and mechanism, and consider inhalation injury.

Appearance

Typically red, dry and painful with no blistering. In RP, describe the affected area, colour and pain rather than assuming a precise depth from appearance alone.

RP assessment

  • Mechanism and time of injury
  • Location and approximate extent
  • Pain score and associated injuries
  • Check for facial/airway involvement and other trauma

RP equipment

Gloves, cooling/water access, clean dressings, cling film where appropriate, analgesia and observation equipment. Escalate if the extent or location is concerning.

Appearance

Partial-thickness burns may be blistered, moist and very painful. Record the appearance and extent and avoid popping blisters in routine RP.

RP assessment

  • Estimate affected body surface area
  • Record depth and distribution
  • Assess circulation and sensation where relevant
  • Check for circumferential, facial, hand, foot or genital involvement

RP equipment

Non-adherent dressings, sterile gauze, cling film for initial cover where appropriate, PPE, pain/observation equipment and a burns referral/handover sheet.

Appearance

Full-thickness burns can look white, brown or charred and may have reduced sensation. Depth can be mixed, so specialist assessment is important.

RP priorities

  • ABCDE and associated trauma first
  • Estimate extent and document location
  • Assess airway/inhalation injury risk
  • Prevent hypothermia and arrange urgent specialist care

RP equipment

Full PPE, sterile/non-adherent coverings, thermal blankets, monitoring equipment, IV-access supplies and specialist burns transfer equipment.

Superficial

Red and painful skin without deeper tissue destruction. Assess extent and symptoms.

Partial thickness

Can be blistered, moist and painful; needs appropriate assessment and dressing.

Full thickness

May appear white, brown or charred and can have reduced sensation. Requires specialist assessment.

Face / airway

High concern for inhalation injury. Assess airway and breathing early and escalate.

Hands / feet / genitals

Functionally important areas; lower threshold for specialist assessment.

Chemical / electrical

Potentially serious. Chemical burns need immediate decontamination/irrigation and emergency assessment; electrical burns can hide deeper injury.

Initial burn care

  1. Stop the burning process and ensure scene safety.
  2. Cool with cool running water for around 20 minutes where appropriate.
  3. Remove nearby jewellery/clothing unless stuck to the skin.
  4. Cover appropriately after cooling and avoid creams/oils.
  5. Assess size, depth, location and inhalation risk.

Pain management

Mild (1-3/10): Paracetamol 1g oral or ibuprofen 400mg. Moderate (4-6/10): Oral morphine 10-20mg or codeine 30-60mg. Severe (7-10/10): IV morphine 2.5-10mg titrated, or IV fentanyl 25-50mcg. Consider Entonox for procedural analgesia during burn dressing changes. Reassess pain score 15 min after each dose.

Observations & monitoring

Record a full set of baseline observations: HR, BP, RR, SpO₂, GCS, temperature, pain score. Monitor for hypothermia — burns can cause rapid heat loss. Repeat observations after every intervention and at least every 10 minutes. Watch for signs of airway deterioration if inhalation injury is suspected.

Transport & handover

SBAR handover:

  1. Situation — burn injury, mechanism (fire/scald/chemical/electrical), time since injury
  2. Background — medical history, allergies, tetanus status
  3. Assessment — %TBSA, depth, location, inhalation injury signs, observations, treatment given
  4. Recommendation — burns unit referral if >10% TBSA, facial/airway burns, circumferential burns, or burns in children

Hospital RP

Expect pain management, wound assessment/cleaning, dressings, fluids for significant burns where indicated, specialist burns input and possible surgery for severe injuries. Airway swelling can evolve, so anticipate difficult airway management if inhalation injury is present.

Smoke / Inhalation Injury

Smoke inhalation can injure the airway and lungs and may accompany major burns.

Look for

  • Cough or wheeze
  • Shortness of breath
  • Hoarse voice or voice change
  • Facial burns
  • Soot around the nose/mouth
  • Confusion or reduced consciousness
  • Increasing work of breathing

Immediate priorities

  1. Remove from smoke exposure when safe to do so.
  2. Assess airway and breathing immediately — high-flow oxygen via non-rebreather mask at 15 L/min.
  3. Monitor SpO2, respiratory rate, heart rate and consciousness level continuously.
  4. Assess for associated burns or other traumatic injuries.
  5. Escalate urgently for suspected inhalation injury — airway swelling can evolve rapidly.

Observations & monitoring

Record a full set of baseline observations: HR, BP, RR, SpO2, GCS, temperature. Monitor SpO2 response to oxygen — failure to improve is a red flag. Watch for stridor, increasing respiratory distress, hoarseness or declining consciousness. Repeat observations every 5 minutes and document trends.

Transport & handover

SBAR handover:

  1. Situation — smoke inhalation, enclosed space exposure, duration of exposure
  2. Background — medical history (especially asthma/COPD), allergies
  3. Assessment — airway signs, SpO2, respiratory rate, associated burns, GCS, treatment given
  4. Recommendation — respiratory assessment, chest imaging, monitoring for delayed deterioration, burns unit if facial burns present

Important

A normal-looking early presentation does not rule out significant inhalation injury. Specialist assessment may be required because airway/lung injury can evolve. Even patients who appear well after rescue should be advised to seek medical attention if symptoms develop.

Allergic Reactions

Separate mild/local reactions from anaphylaxis, where airway, breathing or circulation problems can occur.

Food

Peanuts/tree nuts, milk, egg, fish, shellfish, wheat and other foods can trigger reactions.

Medication

Medicines can cause allergic reactions ranging from rash to anaphylaxis.

Insect sting

Wasps, bees and other stinging insects can trigger severe reactions.

Latex

May cause skin symptoms or, rarely, severe systemic reactions.

Cosmetics / chemicals

Can cause contact reactions; distinguish local skin reactions from systemic anaphylaxis.

Unknown trigger

Treat the clinical presentation and obtain the history once immediate life threats are being managed.

Mild reaction

Possible itching, hives or local swelling without airway, breathing or circulation compromise. Monitor and escalate if symptoms progress.

Anaphylaxis management

Use ABCDE, call for help early. If airway, breathing or circulation are compromised: IM adrenaline 1:1000 (0.5mg) into the anterolateral thigh — this is the first-line treatment. Position the patient appropriately (lying flat with legs raised if shocked, sitting up if respiratory distress). Administer high-flow oxygen and monitor continuously. Repeat adrenaline after 5 minutes if no improvement.

What to monitor

Airway swelling, voice change, wheeze/stridor, breathing difficulty, pulse, BP, consciousness and progression of symptoms. Watch for biphasic reactions — improvement followed by deterioration — even after initial treatment. Record observations every 5 minutes.

Transport & handover

SBAR handover:

  1. Situation — allergic reaction, suspected trigger, onset time, severity
  2. Background — known allergies, previous anaphylaxis, asthma, medications
  3. Assessment — airway/breathing/circulation status, observations, treatment given (including adrenaline dose and time), response
  4. Recommendation — emergency department assessment, observation for biphasic reaction, anaphylaxis care plan

Hospital RP

Expect continued monitoring, IV access, antihistamines and steroids as adjuncts (not replacements for adrenaline), and a period of observation (typically 6-12 hours) due to the risk of biphasic reactions. All patients with anaphylaxis should be assessed in an emergency department.

Water Rescue / Near-Drowning

Get the patient out of the water safely first — resuscitation, warming and monitoring for delayed deterioration come next.

Look for

  • Unresponsiveness or absent/abnormal breathing
  • Coughing, choking or vomiting
  • Blue or pale skin
  • Signs of hypothermia — shivering, confusion, slurred speech
  • Altered consciousness
  • Associated injuries from diving, rocks or entrapment

Immediate priorities

  1. Don't become a second casualty — use reach, throw, row or wade techniques before entering the water yourself.
  2. Remove the patient from the water as safely and quickly as possible.
  3. Check responsiveness and breathing for no more than 10 seconds.
  4. If not breathing, give 5 initial rescue breaths before starting compressions, given the likely hypoxic cause.
  5. Remove wet clothing and begin gentle rewarming — avoid rapid rewarming or rough handling.
  6. Protect the airway if vomiting or not fully alert.
  7. Give high-flow oxygen and monitor SpO2, pulse, BP and temperature continuously.

Observations & monitoring

Record a full set of baseline observations: HR, BP, RR, SpO2, GCS, temperature, pain score. Monitor for delayed deterioration — aspiration pneumonitis or pulmonary oedema can develop hours after an apparently good recovery. Repeat observations at least every 10 minutes. Watch for coughing, wheeze, increasing respiratory rate or declining SpO2.

Transport & handover

SBAR handover:

  1. Situation — water rescue/near-drowning, submersion time, water type (fresh/salt/cold)
  2. Background — medical history (epilepsy, cardiac conditions, diabetes), medications
  3. Assessment — consciousness level, breathing status, temperature, observations, treatment given, response
  4. Recommendation — chest assessment/imaging for aspiration, oxygen therapy, warming, monitoring for delayed pulmonary oedema, possible admission for observation

Hospital RP

Expect chest assessment/imaging for aspiration, oxygen therapy, warming, continuous monitoring and possible admission for observation after any significant submersion. Even patients who appear well after rescue should be observed for several hours due to risk of delayed deterioration.

Animal Attack — Bite & Claw Injuries

Treat the wounds and control bleeding first — puncture wounds can hide deeper damage than they appear to on the surface. Identify the animal species when possible — domestic (dog/cat) vs wild carries different infection risks.

Puncture bite wounds

Deep, narrow wounds that can hide damage to muscle, tendon or vessels, with a high infection risk from contamination. Do not close in the field — leave open for surgical irrigation.

Claw lacerations

Long, often parallel tears that can be deeper than they first appear. Control bleeding, then clean and cover. Assess for underlying muscle or tendon exposure.

Scalp / face

Bleeds heavily due to good blood supply; cosmetic and functional concern needing urgent assessment. Protect the airway if there is facial swelling or bleeding into the mouth.

Neck

High-risk region — major vessel and airway proximity. Treat as an emergency and escalate rapidly. Apply haemostatic dressing and direct pressure if bleeding.

Limb

Assess for deep tissue/tendon damage; check distal circulation, sensation and movement. Consider tourniquet for life-threatening limb haemorrhage. Splint if fracture suspected.

Multiple wounds

Search systematically for every bite/claw site while keeping life-threat priorities first. Animals often attack multiple areas in a single incident — do not stop after finding one wound.

Immediate priorities

  1. Confirm the animal threat has been neutralised or has left before approaching.
  2. Perform a primary survey and control any catastrophic bleeding first — direct pressure, haemostatic gauze or tourniquet as appropriate.
  3. Expose and examine all bite/claw wounds systematically, including less obvious areas (scalp, armpits, behind knees).
  4. Check distal circulation, sensation and movement for any limb wounds — reassess after any intervention.
  5. Irrigate and clean wounds once bleeding is controlled, using warmed saline where available — do not close puncture wounds.
  6. Offer appropriate analgesia — paracetamol/ibuprofen for mild pain, consider oral morphine or IV opioids for significant pain.
  7. Monitor for shock — tachycardia, hypotension, pallor — especially with significant bleeding or multiple wounds.
  8. Escalate for wound exploration, surgical closure and infection-risk management; document the animal species.

Pain management

Mild pain (1-3/10): Paracetamol 1g oral or ibuprofen 400mg oral. Moderate pain (4-6/10): Oral morphine 10-20mg or codeine 30-60mg. Severe pain (7-10/10): IV morphine 2.5-10mg titrated, or IV fentanyl 25-50mcg. Reassess pain score 15 minutes after each dose. Consider Entonox (50:50 nitrous oxide/oxygen) for procedural analgesia during wound cleaning.

Observations & monitoring

Record a full set of baseline observations: HR, BP, RR, SpO₂, GCS, pain score, temperature, capillary refill. Repeat observations after each intervention and at least every 10 minutes in significant trauma. Watch for:

  • Tachycardia or hypotension suggesting haemorrhage
  • Rising pain score suggesting worsening injury
  • Fever developing over time suggesting infection
  • Neurological changes if bites are near the head or neck

Transport & handover

SBAR handover to receiving unit:

  1. Situation — animal attack, species if known, time since incident
  2. Background — tetanus status, allergies, anticoagulants, immunocompromise
  3. Assessment — wound locations, depth estimates, distal function, vital signs, pain score, shock signs
  4. Recommendation — wound exploration, surgical washout, tetanus booster, rabies assessment if wild animal, IV antibiotics

Transport to a trauma unit with surgical capabilities. Document whether the animal was domestic (dog/cat) or wild (bat, raccoon, fox) — rabies risk requires public health notification.

Infection risk

Animal bites carry a high infection risk from oral flora. Key considerations:

  • Dog bites: Pasteurella, Staphylococcus, Streptococcus — co-amoxiclav is first-line prophylaxis
  • Cat bites: Pasteurella multocida — deep puncture wounds, high infection rate — co-amoxiclav or doxycycline
  • Wild animal bites: Rabies risk assessment needed — document the animal, notify public health
  • Tetanus: Check status — booster if >5 years since last dose or unknown
Do not close puncture wounds in the field — they must be surgically irrigated and left open to heal.

Hospital RP

Expect wound exploration under local or general anaesthetic, copious irrigation (at least 1L per wound), surgical debridement of non-viable tissue, possible closure delay, IV antibiotics, tetanus booster if indicated, rabies post-exposure prophylaxis if wild animal, and observation for deeper structural injury (tendon, nerve, vessel). Analgesia will be continued and escalated as needed. Some patients may need admission for IV antibiotics and monitoring.

Fall From Height

Height and landing surface change the risk, but treat as major trauma with spinal precautions until proven otherwise.

Low height~<2m

Still consider fractures, head injury and soft-tissue injury depending on the landing surface.

Moderate height~2–4m

Higher concern for lower-limb, spinal or pelvic fractures and head injury.

High height~4m+

Major trauma mechanism — consider spinal injury, pelvic/lower-limb fractures and internal injury.

Landing positionFeet vs head/back

Feet-first transmits force up through legs/pelvis/spine. Landing on the head or back raises spinal cord injury risk significantly.

Look for

  • Spinal tenderness, deformity or neurological symptoms
  • Pelvic instability or pain
  • Lower-limb deformity or inability to weight-bear
  • Head injury signs, reduced consciousness
  • Chest/abdominal tenderness suggesting internal injury

Immediate priorities

  1. Confirm the scene is safe, including any ongoing fall risk, before approaching.
  2. Approach with spinal precautions given the mechanism, minimising unnecessary movement.
  3. Use <C>ABCDE and control any catastrophic bleeding first.
  4. Gently assess the pelvis without excessive manipulation.
  5. Splint any fractures identified and recheck neurovascular status.
  6. Record full observations and reassess frequently — watch for trends.
  7. Escalate urgently for suspected spinal, pelvic or multi-system injury.

Pain management

Mild (1-3/10): Paracetamol 1g oral or ibuprofen 400mg. Moderate (4-6/10): Oral morphine 10-20mg or codeine 30-60mg. Severe (7-10/10): IV morphine 2.5-10mg titrated. Consider Entonox for procedural analgesia during splinting or logrolling. Reassess pain score 15 min after each dose.

Transport & handover

SBAR handover:

  1. Situation — fall from height, estimated height, landing surface, body position on landing
  2. Background — medical history, anticoagulants, allergies
  3. Assessment — injuries found, spinal/pelvic/limb assessment, observations (HR/BP/RR/SpO2/GCS/pain), treatment given, response
  4. Recommendation — trauma team, spinal imaging, surgical opinion for fractures or internal injury

BP & observations

As with any major trauma mechanism, there's no reliable formula linking fall height to BP. Record a full set of observations and repeat them regularly, watching the trend rather than a single reading.

👊

Melee / Physical Assault

Blunt force can hide serious internal injury behind a normal-looking exterior — don't be reassured by a lack of visible wounds.

Blunt force to head

Consider concussion/traumatic brain injury. Assess GCS and pupils, and watch closely for deterioration.

Blows to chest

Consider rib fracture or pulmonary contusion. Watch breathing rate and effort closely.

Blows to abdomen

Consider internal organ injury or bleeding even without external marks. Reassess repeatedly.

Blunt weapon strikes

Bats, pipes or similar can cause fractures and deep bruising depending on force and weapon.

Strangulation / neck trauma

High-risk injury — airway, vascular and cervical-spine concerns. Escalate urgently even if the patient looks well.

Prolonged assault

Multiple assailants or a sustained attack raise the chance of multiple injury sites and higher cumulative energy transfer.

Immediate priorities

  1. Confirm the scene is safe and any assailant risk has passed before approaching.
  2. Use <C>ABCDE and control any catastrophic bleeding first.
  3. Assess level of consciousness and look for signs of head injury.
  4. Fully expose the patient and check systematically for bruising, swelling and deformity.
  5. Carefully assess the neck for any signs of strangulation.
  6. Repeat observations regularly and escalate urgently for any deterioration.

Pain management

Mild (1-3/10): Paracetamol 1g oral or ibuprofen 400mg. Moderate (4-6/10): Oral morphine 10-20mg or codeine 30-60mg. Severe (7-10/10): IV morphine 2.5-10mg titrated. Consider Entonox for procedural analgesia. Reassess pain score 15 min after each dose.

Observations & monitoring

Record a full set of baseline observations: HR, BP, RR, SpO2, GCS, pain score, temperature. Repeat observations after every intervention and at least every 10 minutes. Watch for signs of deterioration — rising pain score, tachycardia, hypotension, neurological changes, or abdominal tenderness suggesting hidden internal injury.

Transport & handover

SBAR handover:

  1. Situation — assault, mechanism (fists/weapon), number of assailants/blows if known
  2. Background — medical history, anticoagulants, allergies
  3. Assessment — injuries found, head/neck/chest/abdomen/limb assessment, observations, treatment given, response
  4. Recommendation — trauma assessment, imaging as indicated, wound management, monitoring for delayed deterioration

Important

Blunt trauma can cause serious internal injury with minimal external signs. Don't let a patient who "looks fine" delay a full assessment. Reassess repeatedly — internal injury can evolve over time.

HART — Hazardous Area Response Team

Specialist ambulance capability for patients in hazardous or technically challenging environments. Exact capabilities and deployment arrangements vary by ambulance trust.

Common HART-type activations

  • CBRN / HazMat incidents
  • Urban search and rescue / structural collapse
  • Safe working at height
  • High-risk confined space
  • Inland water operations
  • Tactical medicine / marauding terrorist incidents
  • Complex hazardous-area casualties
  • Mass-casualty / major-incident support where specialist capability is required

  • Specialist PPE and hazardous-area capability
  • Support to CBRN/HazMat response and decontamination activity
  • Interoperable working with police, fire and other agencies
  • Specialist access/rescue environments

  1. Control identifies specialist need
  2. HART requested / tasked
  3. Incident location and hazards confirmed
  4. Joint safety arrangements established
  5. HART team deploys to the appropriate operational area
  6. Casualty access / clinical care represented in RP
  7. Casualty transferred to safe treatment/transport area
  8. Handover and demobilisation

HEMS — Helicopter Emergency Medical Service

Specialist pre-hospital critical-care resource. Activation criteria, clinical scope and availability vary by service.

Common HEMS-type calls

  • Major trauma with suspected time-critical injury
  • Serious road traffic collisions, especially entrapment/ejection
  • Severe falls
  • Penetrating trauma such as stabbing or shooting
  • Severe head injury
  • Drowning / near drowning
  • Electrocution
  • Industrial accidents
  • Explosions
  • Traumatic amputation
  • Remote/difficult-access incidents
  • Requests from ambulance, police or fire colleagues where specialist critical care may help

What HEMS adds

  • Rapid specialist critical-care team response
  • Advanced pre-hospital decision-making and interventions within local scope
  • Potential rapid access to major trauma/definitive care pathways
  • Specialist scene assessment and critical-care support

HEMS scene workflow — RP

  1. Potentially suitable case identified
  2. HEMS requested / tasked
  3. Landing/access information exchanged
  4. Landing site or alternative access arranged
  5. HEMS crew meets ground team
  6. Joint assessment and treatment plan represented
  7. Transport / rendezvous / handover decision
  8. Definitive-care handover

Think HART when…

The environment is the problem. Hazardous materials, CBRN, technical rescue, confined space, height, water or tactical/hazardous access.

Think HEMS when…

The patient's clinical need and time/access are the problem. Major trauma, severe injury, time-critical critical care or difficult geography.

The environment and patient are both high-risk. For example, a severe trauma patient trapped in a hazardous/technical environment.

High-risk surgical removal of a projectile from the head or neck region. Requires neurosurgical or vascular surgical expertise.

Indications

Intracranial or cervical bullet fragment causing mass effect, infection risk, or vascular compromise. Emergency surgery for haemodynamic instability or neurological deterioration.

  • Microsurgical instruments for neurovascular dissection

  • Image guidance / intraoperative X-ray if available
  • Blood products cross-matched and available

Active Haemorrhage Control - Damage Control Surgery

Emergency surgery to stop bleeding and contamination quickly before definitive repair.

Indications

Haemodynamic instability from suspected intra-abdominal, intrathoracic or retroperitoneal haemorrhage.

Principles

  1. Control haemorrhage - ligate, clamp, pack or repair
  2. Control contamination - staple or ligate bowel perforations
  3. Pack the abdomen/chest if diffuse bleeding
  4. Temporary closure - leave open, plan second-look in 24-48 hours
  5. ICU resuscitation before definitive repair

Equipment

  • Emergency laparotomy/thoracotomy tray
  • Vascular clamps, aortic compressor, REBOA catheter
  • Laparotomy pads for packing, haemostatic gauze
  • Massive transfusion protocol activated
  • Rapid infuser, warming devices
  • Temporary closure device (Bogota bag) if leaving open

Wound Debridement & Closure

Surgical cleaning of a contaminated wound - removing dead tissue and foreign material.

Indications

Open fractures, contaminated lacerations, crush injuries, gunshot wounds, animal bites, necrotising fasciitis.

Principles

  1. Excise all devitalised tissue
  2. Remove all foreign material - irrigate with copious warmed saline
  3. Assess deep structures - tendons, nerves, vessels, bone
  4. Leave wound open if contaminated - planned second-look in 24-48 hours
  5. Delayed primary closure when the wound is clean

Equipment

  • Debridement tray - scalpel, scissors, forceps, retractors
  • High-pressure irrigation system
  • Warmed saline - at least 3-6 litres
  • Haemostatic agents, diathermy
  • Culture swabs, wound photography
  • Negative pressure wound therapy if available

Emergency Field Surgery - On Scene

Life-saving procedures performed in the field when there is no time to reach a hospital. These are damage control procedures only - the patient will need definitive surgical care in theatre.

When to operate in the field

Only when the patient will die before reaching hospital. This includes: uncontrollable external haemorrhage, tension pneumothorax not relieved by needle decompression, open pneumothorax with bubbling, or traumatic amputation with uncontrolled bleeding. Field surgery is a last resort.

Field surgery kit

  • Scalpel, scissors, forceps, needle holder, retractors
  • Haemostatic gauze (QuickClot/Celox), tourniquets
  • Chest tube / thoracostomy kit, occlusive dressings
  • IV access supplies, warmed saline
  • Sterile gloves, gowns, masks, eye protection
  • Head torch / portable lighting
  • Emergency blanket, thermal protection
  • Basic wound closure supplies, stapler, sutures

Key principles

  1. Only do what is necessary to save life - no cosmetic surgery
  2. Control haemorrhage first - clamp, pack, tourniquet
  3. Deal with tension pneumothorax - finger thoracostomy or chest tube
  4. Cover open wounds with sterile dressings
  5. Splint fractures with available materials
  6. Get the patient to definitive care as fast as possible
  7. Document what was done for handover to the surgical team

Skin Graft

Reconstructive surgery used to cover a suitable wound or burn area with a graft. Use the theatre workflow for RP and follow the surgical team's definitive plan.

Indications

Suitable wounds after appropriate assessment and preparation, including selected burns, traumatic skin loss and wounds requiring coverage.

Preparation

  • Confirm patient, graft site and recipient site
  • Review wound condition, imaging and relevant observations
  • Confirm consent/status, allergies and peri-operative plan
  • Prepare recipient-site and donor-site equipment
  • Check sterile packs, graft instruments, dressings and packaging

Equipment

  • Skin graft / plastic surgery tray
  • Dermatome and graft handling instruments
  • Mesher and carrier supplies where required
  • Sterile marking, measuring and dressing supplies
  • Donor-site and recipient-site dressings
  • Specimen/container supplies if required

Start → Finish

  1. Briefing and patient/site verification
  2. Positioning, preparation and sterile draping
  3. Team time-out and equipment check
  4. Prepare the recipient wound and donor site as directed
  5. Harvest and prepare the graft using the selected technique
  6. Apply and secure the graft
  7. Dress donor and recipient sites
  8. Complete counts, documentation and recovery handover

RP Actions

/me confirms the donor and recipient sites with the theatre team.
/me checks the graft tray, dermatome, dressings and sterile packs.
/me verifies sterile packaging and equipment integrity before opening supplies.
/me assists with positioning, preparation and the theatre time-out.
/me prepares the graft equipment and records the graft site and key findings.
/me applies the planned dressings and confirms counts with the scrub team.
/me documents the procedure and gives a structured recovery handover.

Post-Operative Handover

Document graft and donor sites, dressings, observations, analgesia/recovery plan and any instructions from the surgical team. Escalate unexpected bleeding, deterioration or other complications promptly.
